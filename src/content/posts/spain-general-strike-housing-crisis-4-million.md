Spain came to a partial standstill on Wednesday, November 11, as about 4 million workers joined a 24-hour general strike over the country's housing crisis, according to the unions that organised it. It was the first nationwide general strike in Spain since 2012, when workers walked out against austerity during the euro zone debt crisis. The protest was driven by anger over rising rents, evictions and a shortage of homes, and it comes less than three weeks before a snap general election on November 29.

## Who Called the Strike and How Many Joined

The walkout was backed by UGT and CCOO, Spain's two largest trade unions, which together represent more than a million workers, along with tenants' associations. CCOO said more than 4,500 union branches and works councils had publicly endorsed the strike before it began at midnight.

Unions put participation at around 4 million people by the afternoon, roughly one fifth of Spain's workforce of about 23 million, and said they expected the figure to reach 5 million by the end of the day. These are union estimates and have not been independently confirmed.

Turnout was uneven. Reuters reported that support was strongest in coastal regions, where pressure on housing is most severe. Unions said participation hit 90% at ports and in the car industry, with full stoppages at Volkswagen's plant in Navarre and SEAT's factory in Barcelona. Hospitality and finance, two fast growing but less unionised sectors, were less affected.

## Signs of the Shutdown Across Cities

The strike left visible marks on daily life:

- **Electricity:** Grid operator Red Electrica said power demand fell by as much as 15.5% between 10 am and 11 am local time.
- **Metro services:** Barcelona's metro ran no trains outside peak commuting hours, while Madrid's metro reported peak hour use down 42% compared with the same period a week earlier.
- **Shops:** Big fashion stores on Madrid's Gran Via, including Primark, H&M and Pull&Bear, pulled down their shutters. Many shops in Barcelona closed and boarded up their fronts.
- **Weather:** Two storms, Charles and Bruna, disrupted the day, and unions called off afternoon marches in Cadiz and other southern cities.

Before the strike, the <a href="https://es.usembassy.gov/alert-general-strike-in-spain-on-november-11-2026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">US Embassy in Madrid warned travellers</a> that public transport would run only minimum services and that museums, shops, restaurants and pharmacies could be closed.

### Minimum Services That Stayed in Place

Spanish law requires essential services to keep running during strikes. According to The Olive Press, national rail operator Renfe was set to run 75% of commuter trains at peak hours, 65% of medium distance services and 73% of high speed AVE and long distance trains. Hospitals kept emergency, intensive care and maternity units open, though routine appointments and non urgent operations could be cancelled. Airports stayed open, with flights to the Balearic and Canary Islands, Ceuta and Melilla among the protected routes.

## Clashes and Arrests in Madrid and Barcelona

The day was largely peaceful, according to the unions, but tensions rose in the evening. In Barcelona, clashes broke out between police and some protesters after the main march, and furniture was set on fire. In Madrid, riot police used foam bullets, tear gas and pepper spray to disperse crowds near Plaza de Espana, Reuters reported.

Police said at least 73 people had been arrested nationwide by 21:00 GMT, linked to at least 1,044 incidents, including blockades of offices and industrial estates and burning rubbish bins. Unions said 20 protesters were hurt in a police operation using tear gas and batons and said they would file a criminal complaint, calling the response "disproportionate." The Interior Ministry had not immediately commented.

## Why Housing Has Become Spain's Biggest Grievance

The numbers behind the anger are stark. Spain's central bank estimates the country is short of about 750,000 homes, a gap it expects to pass 1 million by 2028. Mortgage rates have climbed to their highest level in several years.

Weeks of protests had built up after an 87 year old woman in Madrid died following her eviction. One 36 year old striker in Madrid told Reuters he still could not afford to move out on his own.

Not everyone backed the walkout. Angela de Miguel, vice president of the business lobby CEOE, called the strike counterproductive and argued that the housing shortage also hurts small firms by making hiring harder. She said the answer lies in building more homes and cutting taxes on construction and home purchases.

## The Economic Cost and the November 29 Election

Miguel Cardoso, chief economist at BBVA Research, estimated that the strike could reduce Spain's annual GDP by 0.1% to 0.2%. He noted that remote working and the country's services heavy economy may limit the damage compared with earlier general strikes.

The strike has a clear political backdrop. Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez called the snap election after parliament last month rejected government decrees meant to curb evictions and cap rent rises. According to Reuters, Sanchez hopes voter frustration over housing will help his Socialist Party, which still trails in opinion polls, and shift attention away from corruption cases involving his inner circle and a deadly train crash last month. International outlets including <a href="https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/11/europe/spain-strike-housing-latam-intl" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CNN covered the walkout</a> as Spain's first general strike in 14 years.

## What Comes Next for Spain

With the election set for November 29, housing is now at the centre of the campaign. Unions have shown they can bring millions onto the streets, business groups are pushing for more construction and lower taxes, and the government is betting that the issue works in its favour. Whoever forms the next government will face the same basic problem: too few homes and rents that eat up almost the entire pay packet of many young Spaniards.
