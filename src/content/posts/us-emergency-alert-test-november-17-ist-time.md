Televisions and radios across the United States will briefly go quiet on Tuesday, November 17, 2026, for a one-minute national test of the Emergency Alert System (EAS). The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) will send the test with the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) at 2:20 pm Eastern Time, which is 12:50 am on Wednesday, November 18, in India. It is the first nationwide test since October 2023, and it arrives as viral posts on TikTok wrongly claim it marks the start of a 90-day blackout.

Here is what the test is, what it will look like, why mobile phones are left out this year, and how it compares with the alert system India has been testing on its own networks.

## What Viewers and Listeners Will See

Broadcasters, cable systems, satellite TV and satellite radio services will interrupt normal programming for about one minute. Viewers will hear the familiar EAS attention tones, followed by a message saying this is a nationwide test issued by FEMA, that it is only a test, and that no action is required by the public. Some stations may run the message in both English and Spanish.

The alert travels through FEMA's Integrated Public Alert and Warning System (IPAWS) as a Common Alerting Protocol message using the special "NPT" code reserved for a Nationwide Test of the EAS, according to a compliance note from the law firm <a href="https://www.wiley.law/alert-Nationwide-EAS-Test-Coming-November-17-2026" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Wiley</a>. Stations that miss FEMA's internet feed can still pick up the alert over the air from other stations, a backup path FEMA describes as part of the system's built-in redundancy.

If a real emergency or serious weather event hits on test day, FEMA will push the test to December 3, 2026.

## Why Phones Will Stay Silent This Time

The 2023 test went to both broadcast outlets and mobile phones through Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA). This year's test is EAS only, so phones in the US are not expected to buzz. Trade publication TV Technology reported that the November test "has no Wireless Emergency Alert component". FEMA has not publicly explained in the reports we reviewed why WEA was left out.

FEMA says federal law requires it to test the national public alert system at least once every three years. The tones themselves date back to 1963, when President John F. Kennedy set up the original Emergency Broadcast System, and more than 1,700 local, state, tribal and territorial authorities use the same system for local warnings.

## The Blackout Claim Does Not Hold Up

In the days before the test, TikTok posts claimed a nationwide 90-day blackout would begin on November 17 and urged people to stockpile food and water. One clip appeared to show President Donald Trump announcing it. Fact-checking site <a href="https://politifact.com/factchecks/2026/nov/16/tiktok-posts/blackout-nov-17-trump-us-emergency/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PolitiFact rated the claim False</a>, finding that the Trump footage came from a November 9 news conference in which he never mentioned a blackout. No government agency has announced any blackout.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told Fox News that Americans do not need to stockpile supplies, and called the online panic unfounded. PolitiFact also noted that some posts mixed in unrelated events, such as last week's <a href="/microsoft-outlook-teams-outage-november-12/">Microsoft outage</a> and the <a href="/faa-ground-stop-lifted-thousands-flights-cancelled/">FAA flight ground stop</a>. Microsoft said its outage was a technical fault and not a cyberattack, and flights resumed on November 13. A separate Pentagon programme to keep military bases on their own power for 90 days is a preparedness plan, not a forecast.

## Test Timings in IST and Key Deadlines

For readers in India, or families tracking relatives in different US time zones, here is the schedule converted from Eastern Time. The US is on standard time in November, so Eastern Time is 10 hours and 30 minutes behind IST.

| Item | US time | India time (IST) |
|---|---|---|
| National EAS test | Nov 17, 2:20 pm ET (1:20 pm CT, 11:20 am PT) | Nov 18, 12:50 am |
| Length of test | About 1 minute | About 1 minute |
| Backup test date | Dec 3, 2:20 pm ET | Dec 4, 12:50 am |
| Broadcasters' day-of-test report (ETRS Form Two) | Nov 19, 2:20 pm ET | Nov 20, 12:50 am |
| Detailed post-test report (ETRS Form Three) | Jan 4, 2027 | Jan 4, 2027 |

The ETRS filings are for broadcasters and cable operators, which must report to the FCC whether they received and passed on the alert. Ordinary viewers do not need to file or do anything.

## How the US Test Compares With India's Cell Broadcast Alerts

India took a different route this year. On May 2, 2026, the Department of Telecommunications ran a nationwide test of its indigenous Cell Broadcast Alert system, built with the National Disaster Management Authority, and millions of mobile phones received a test message, as <a href="https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2026/05/02/received-an-extremely-severe-alert-on-phone-india-tests-mobile-based-disaster-communication-system.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The Week reported</a>. That message, like the US one, told the public that no action was needed.

| Feature | US test, Nov 17, 2026 | India test, May 2, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Run by | FEMA with the FCC | Department of Telecommunications with NDMA |
| Main channel | TV, radio, cable, satellite | Mobile phones in targeted areas |
| Phones included | No | Yes |
| Languages | English and Spanish | English, Hindi and regional languages |
| Message standard | Common Alerting Protocol | Common Alerting Protocol (ITU recommended) |
| Coverage | All US states and territories | All 36 states and union territories |

Both systems use the same international message format, which lets one alert reach many kinds of devices. The big difference is the target: the US test checks its broadcast backbone, while India's test checked whether phones in a chosen area light up at once during cyclones, floods or other disasters.

## What Indians in the US Should Keep in Mind

Indian students, workers and visitors in the US do not need to do anything. If a TV or radio suddenly plays loud tones and a test message around 2:20 pm Eastern on Tuesday, that is expected. Since phones are not part of this test, a phone alert on that day would not be the national test and should be read carefully, as it could be a genuine local warning. Families in India who see alarming forwards about a US blackout can safely ignore them; the only scheduled event is a one-minute broadcast check.

The test is routine maintenance for a warning system that most people only notice when something goes wrong. Station reports filed over the coming weeks will show where it needs repair.
