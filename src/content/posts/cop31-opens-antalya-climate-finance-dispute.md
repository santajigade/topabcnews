The United Nations climate summit COP31 opened in Antalya, Turkey, on Monday, November 9, but not on time. The opening ceremony started about an hour and a half late, with no official reason given, after negotiators spent Sunday night arguing over whether climate finance items demanded by developing countries should be placed on the formal agenda. The delay set the tone for a two-week meeting where money, not targets, is shaping up to be the hardest question.

Thousands of delegates, officials and observers have gathered at a large expo centre in the Mediterranean resort city. Turkey holds the COP31 presidency, while Australia leads the negotiations under a power-sharing deal that ended a long dispute over who would host this year's talks. In our earlier preview, we explained <a href="/cop31-antalya-climate-summit-india/">what is at stake for India at COP31</a>. This report covers what actually happened on day one.

## Why the Opening Ceremony Was Delayed

The hold-up came from a familiar fault line in climate diplomacy. Developing nations pushed for finance items to be written into the official agenda, since anything left off the agenda is much harder to negotiate into a final decision. According to a source quoted by Arab News, a "climate finance work programme" will be included.

African countries had also wanted a separate agenda point on tripling adaptation finance, which is money that helps poorer nations cope with floods, droughts and heat that are already happening. That request did not get its own item. The source said it would instead be discussed under other agenda headings.

Jasper Inventor, who heads Greenpeace's COP31 delegation, said wealthy countries had taken a hard line on including a platform from COP30 for discussing how rich nations should fund poorer ones. "This has already been a difficult start for COP31," he said. A senior European Union official also warned that there was no political will for fresh funding pledges at this summit.

## What Guterres, Kurum and Bowen Told Delegates

UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres used the opening to press leaders to cut emissions faster, speed up the move away from fossil fuels and deliver the finance that vulnerable countries need. "We have entered a new age of climate extremes," he said.

Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister and COP31 President, described the meeting as an "Implementation COP" focused on turning existing promises into results. He summed up his approach in three words: dialogue, consensus and action. "We know that delay carries one of the heaviest costs in climate action," Kurum said.

Australian Climate Minister Chris Bowen, who serves as president of negotiations, acknowledged that the world is still on track to overshoot the 1.5 degree Celsius limit set in the Paris Agreement, but said the goal is still worth pursuing. According to Daily Sabah, Bowen pointed to more than 160 agenda items and 60 substantive negotiating mandates that must be handled in two weeks.

### Key highlights from day one

- COP31 opened on November 9 in Antalya, about 90 minutes behind schedule.
- A climate finance work programme will be on the agenda, according to a source.
- Tripling adaptation finance will not get its own agenda item.
- The EU signalled little appetite for new funding pledges.
- Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva is due at the leaders' summit on November 11 and 12.
- The US government has not sent administration officials, though a US congressional delegation is attending.

## The Money Question Hanging Over Antalya

The finance fight is rooted in the deal reached at COP29 in Baku. Under that agreement, developed countries are expected to lead in mobilising at least $300 billion a year for developing countries by 2035, with a wider goal of $1.3 trillion a year from all sources. Poorer nations argue that current flows fall far short of what they need, and that rising disasters make adaptation money urgent.

Turkey has proposed a tool called the Climate Implementation Bridge, or BRIDGE, to help prepare priority projects so they can attract investors. Daily Sabah noted that it is not a dedicated fund, which means it does not by itself add new money. Key technical issues on the table include the Just Transition Mechanism, adaptation finance and Article 2.1(c) of the Paris Agreement, which deals with making overall financial flows consistent with low-emission development. The <a href="https://unfccc.int/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">UN climate secretariat (UNFCCC)</a> publishes official agendas and decision texts as talks progress.

## Turkey's Electrification Push and the Fossil Fuel Debate

The host country's centrepiece is a voluntary "35 by 35" pledge, which aims to raise electricity's share of global energy use from about 20 percent today to 35 percent by 2035. The European Union wants the pledge to focus on "clean" electrification, a wording battle that could reopen the bigger argument over the future of oil, gas and coal.

That argument has been difficult since COP28 in Dubai, where countries agreed to transition away from fossil fuels. The COP30 deal in Brazil did not explicitly mention fossil fuels, and Brazil instead produced a voluntary roadmap. Turkey's wider COP31 Action Agenda covers energy, buildings, industry and waste, including a goal to halve the projected increase in waste generation by 2035. These targets are voluntary and not legally binding under the Paris Agreement.

## A Tense Global Backdrop for the Talks

Observers expect few major breakthroughs. The summit comes during an oil price shock and serious geopolitical strain, including the US war with Iran and Russia's war in Ukraine, as reported by Arab News. The past year also brought heatwaves in Europe, wildfires in Indonesia, floods in Nepal and an exceptionally strong El Nino. In September, the UN warned that global temperatures were on course to exceed the 1.5 degree threshold. The <a href="https://wmo.int/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">World Meteorological Organization</a> has reported that 2026 continued a run of exceptional temperatures.

For countries such as India, which face rising heat and flood risks and depend on affordable finance to expand clean energy, the outcome of the finance debate will matter more than any headline pledge.

## What Comes Next in the Two-Week Summit

Attention now turns to the leaders' segment on November 11 and 12, when heads of state, including Brazil's Lula, are expected to speak. Meanwhile, negotiators will keep working through the long list of agenda items and mandates. Whether the "Implementation COP" can deliver anything beyond voluntary pledges will depend largely on whether rich and poor countries can close the gap on finance over the next two weeks.
