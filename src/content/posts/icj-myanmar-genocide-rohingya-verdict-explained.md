The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday, November 19, that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State during the military's 2017 "clearance operations". Nine of the court's 15 judges backed the finding, and six dissented. It is the first time the United Nations' top court has found a state itself responsible for genocide. The ruling came in a case brought by The Gambia in 2019, and it was read out in The Hague by court President Yuji Iwasawa in a reading that lasted about three hours.

## What the Judges Found About 2017

The court rejected Myanmar's argument that the 2017 campaign was a lawful response to insurgents. According to <a href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/myanmar-genocide-rohingyas-un-top-court/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CBS News</a>, the judges found that security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, carried out widespread sexual violence and extreme violence against children, and attacked people as they fled. The court said the evidence showed about 10,000 Rohingya were killed.

To call this genocide, the judges had to be satisfied that Myanmar intended to destroy the Rohingya as a group, in whole or in part, and that this was the only reasonable inference from the evidence. The majority said it was.

The court did not accept every claim. It held that Myanmar was not responsible for inciting private persons to commit genocide. LiveLaw reported that the judges acknowledged military-linked Facebook accounts had spread anti-Rohingya messages, but found the evidence did not conclusively prove direct appeals to genocide. The court also did not find that Myanmar had deliberately imposed living conditions meant to destroy the group.

## The Ruling, Finding by Finding

The table below sets out the main parts of the judgment as reported by LiveLaw, CBS News and The New Arab.

| Question before the court | Finding |
|---|---|
| Did Myanmar commit genocide in 2017? | Yes, by 9 votes to 6 (killing, serious bodily or mental harm, measures to prevent births) |
| Did it fail to prevent genocide? | Yes |
| Did it fail to punish those responsible? | Yes. Its investigations were not prompt, independent or impartial, and no one was charged with genocide |
| Did it pass laws to apply the Genocide Convention? | No, which the court found was a breach |
| Did the state incite genocide? | Not proven |
| Were genocidal living conditions imposed? | Not proven |

## What Myanmar Must Now Do

Myanmar has to allow and help the safe, voluntary and dignified return of Rohingya who fled, and reunite separated families. It must return or replace lost property, search for people who disappeared, and identify and rebury the dead in line with Rohingya traditions. It must also give injured survivors physical and psychological rehabilitation, protect the Rohingya from discrimination and persecution, and give guarantees that the violations will not happen again.

Myanmar has to investigate and punish those suspected of genocide. It must also report back to the court within two years on what it has done to restore the Rohingya's rights. Where full restitution is not possible, that report must set out how much compensation will be paid and how. No figure was set on Thursday; the court will fix compensation later.

## A Case Seven Years in the Making

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| August 2017 | Military crackdown in Rakhine after attacks on police posts by Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army fighters; more than 730,000 Rohingya flee to Bangladesh |
| November 2019 | The Gambia files the case, backed by the 57-member Organisation of Islamic Cooperation |
| 2019 | Then-leader Aung San Suu Kyi tells the court the accusations are "incomplete and misleading" |
| 2021 | Military seizes power in a coup |
| January 12, 2026 | Full hearings open in The Hague, running about three weeks |
| November 19, 2026 | Judgment delivered |

During the January hearings, Myanmar's representative Ko Ko Hlaing argued that the country "was not obliged to remain idle" against what he called terrorists, The New Arab reported. Senior General Min Aung Hlaing, who led the army in 2017 and took power in the 2021 coup, has since been installed as civilian president after restricted elections.

## How This Compares With Earlier Genocide Cases

The ICJ has come close before. In 2007 it found that Serbia had violated its duty to prevent genocide at Srebrenica in 1995, but it did not find that Serbia as a state committed genocide.

Two other cases under the same convention are still pending. South Africa's case accusing Israel of breaching the Genocide Convention in Gaza is years away from a final ruling, and Ukraine's case against Russia is also open. Legal experts quoted by CBS News and The New Arab expect judges and lawyers in those cases to study how the court treated evidence of intent here. ## Why Enforcement Is the Hard Part

The judgment is final, binding and cannot be appealed. But the ICJ has no police or army. If Myanmar ignores it, The Gambia can take the matter to the UN Security Council, where any of the five permanent members can block action. <a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/18/icj-to-rule-on-rohingya-genocide-case-what-to-expect" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Al Jazeera</a> noted that China and Russia have shielded Myanmar there before. The General Assembly and individual governments can still apply political pressure.

The UN says more than 1.3 million remain displaced across South and Southeast Asia, including about 1.2 million in the crowded camps of Cox's Bazar in Bangladesh, and about 520,000 still live inside Myanmar. The UN refugee agency estimates 3,135 people have died or gone missing at sea since 2022.

Rights activist Yasmin Ullah, who attended the reading, called it a great day for the Rohingya. In a Bangladesh camp, 20-year-old survivor Mohammed Osman told reporters he hoped to go home to perform last rites for relatives killed by the military. Myanmar's first report to the court on returns and restitution is due within two years.
