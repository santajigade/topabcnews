The SS United States, the ocean liner that still holds the transatlantic speed record it set in 1952, now rests on the floor of the Gulf of Mexico. The ship was deliberately sunk off the Florida Panhandle on Wednesday, November 11, in a controlled operation that took several hours. Okaloosa County in Florida, which bought the vessel, plans to turn it into the largest artificial reef in the world, creating new habitat for fish and a major attraction for divers.

## How the Sinking Took Place

The operation began on a cloudy Wednesday afternoon. Organisers had expected the ship to go down in about an hour, but it took far longer. The liner leaned to one side and finally slipped under the surface at around 9 pm local time, according to the <a href="https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/watch-live-ss-united-states-once-worlds-fastest-ocean-liner-sinks-to-become-reef-off-florida" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Associated Press report carried by PBS NewsHour</a>. Officials had moved the date forward by one day over worries about bad weather.

No explosives were used, as federal environmental and explosives rules did not allow them. Instead, crews flooded the ship in a controlled way. They filled 128 fuel and lubricant tanks with water so the hull would settle evenly on the seabed, which slowed the descent.

Okaloosa County spokesperson Nick Tomecek admitted the process "took longer than we thought", adding simply that it is a big ship.

## Where the New Reef Lies

The wreck sits about 25 miles (40 km) southwest of Destin, between Panama City and Pensacola. The water there is about 180 feet deep. Because the ship is so tall, its top deck is only about 60 feet (18 metres) below the surface, which is shallow enough for less experienced divers to visit.

## Why Old Ships Make Good Reefs

Artificial reefs give marine animals hard surfaces to live on and shelter in. Over time, corals, sponges and algae grow on the metal, attracting small fish, which in turn draw larger predators. Organisers say the SS United States will create habitat for species such as tuna, marlin and swordfish, according to AP.

Getting the ship ready took a year of work. <a href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ss-united-states-sinking-ocean-floor-largest-artificial-reef-world-florida-gulf-coast/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CBS News</a> reported that the county sent the vessel to Mobile, Alabama, where workers stripped out materials that could harm the environment, including wiring, fuel and non-metal items. The clean-up was coordinated with the US Environmental Protection Agency and the Florida Department of Environmental Protection. Holes were cut throughout the hull so the ship would stay upright underwater, and AP reported that more than 400 portholes were removed and 10 by 10 foot openings were cut into the decks.

The area already has many such sites. Destin-Fort Walton Beach says it has about 580 artificial reefs, the most of any region in the United States, CBS News said. The previous record holder for the largest artificial reef was the aircraft carrier USS Oriskany, which was sunk off Pensacola in 2006.

## The Liner's Record-Breaking History

Built in Virginia in 1950 and 1951 by more than 3,000 workers, the SS United States was longer than the Titanic by about 108 feet. Its design was so advanced that the Pentagon kept details secret.

On its first voyage in 1952, carrying about 1,700 passengers, it averaged almost 41 miles per hour across the Atlantic. That beat the RMS Queen Mary's 1938 record, and the liner's record still stands today. Over its career it crossed the Atlantic more than 800 times, carrying Hollywood stars such as Grace Kelly and Elizabeth Taylor and US Presidents Truman, Eisenhower and Kennedy.

The ship was retired in 1969. Plans to turn it into a hotel or put it back into cruise service never worked out, and it sat at a dock in Philadelphia for decades.

## Tourism, Diving and a Planned Museum

Okaloosa County bought the ship in 2024 from the SS United States Conservancy for about $1 million, CBS News reported. The overall project has been paid for with tourist tax money. Local businesses expect divers to come year round. Jocelyn Collins of Emerald Coast Scuba told CBS she thinks it will be "like Disney World" for divers.

Not everything went under the water. The ship's 65-foot smokestacks were removed and will anchor a planned museum. Susan Gibbs, granddaughter of the ship's designer, said she was happy that parts of the ship would be saved.
