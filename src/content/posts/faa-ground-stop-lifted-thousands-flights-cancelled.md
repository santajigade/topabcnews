The United States halted almost all departing flights for more than four hours on Friday, November 13, after a telecommunications failure hit the country's air traffic control network. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) ordered a nationwide ground stop at 3:54 pm Eastern Time, citing "extraordinary circumstances and reasons for safety," and lifted it at 8:15 pm. By then more than 4,700 flights had been cancelled and around 10,000 delayed, according to flight tracking data reported by Al Jazeera. Officials say there is no sign of a cyberattack, and attention has turned to ageing equipment and a recent software update.

For Indian readers, the halt happened in the early hours of Saturday, November 14, India time (roughly 2:24 am to 6:45 am IST), and some long-haul and international services connecting through US airports were also caught up in the disruption.

## What Went Wrong Inside the Air Traffic Network

The problem was concentrated in the air route traffic control centres, the large facilities that manage aircraft flying at high altitude between airports. There are 21 such centres, one in Alaska and 20 in the lower 48 states.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said switches and routers in the FAA network became flooded with data and started producing inconsistent information, so the agency shut the system down as a precaution. He said there was no total loss of communications or radar, but officials admitted that new technology being added to the network had overloaded older parts, causing intermittent gaps in communications and radar.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said the failure looked mechanical. He suggested that the FAA's patching and installation of new equipment was overwhelming the older system.

CBS News, citing several sources, reported that the outage followed an update to the private communications network that links the air traffic control system. That network, which it said is operated by L3Harris, rebooted and then crashed. No official root cause has been announced yet.

## Was It a Cyberattack? What Officials Say

Mullin said there was no "chatter" or "footprints" pointing to hackers, though he added that he was not ruling anything out while the investigation continues. Bloomberg, quoting a person familiar with the matter, reported that the FBI does not believe a cyberattack or criminal activity caused the outage and is examining a software update as a possible trigger.

The National Security Agency, the Department of Defense, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and other bodies are taking part in the probe, Mullin said. Officials also said that the large Microsoft outage earlier in the week was unrelated, and that AI systems the FAA recently deployed played no role. ## How Many Flights and Passengers Were Hit

The timing was poor. Data from aviation analytics firm Cirium showed that about 11,000 flights carrying more than 1.5 million passengers were still due to leave US airports after 4 pm on Friday.

Aircraft already in the air were allowed to continue to their destinations, and Mullin said around 3,000 flights landed safely after the order was issued. International departures were handled case by case, and some were cleared to leave. Passengers at Los Angeles International Airport and other hubs described being stranded for hours with little information.

Al Jazeera described the halt as the longest ground stop in 25 years. The previous nationwide ground stop was in January 2023, when a contractor accidentally deleted files on the system that sends safety notices to pilots. No cyberattack was found in that case either.

## When Flights Return to Normal Before Thanksgiving

Departures have resumed, but officials warned that delays will continue for several days. Duffy said flights would restart slowly because airlines need pilots and flight attendants to be in the right places, and he expects normal operations by Thanksgiving, which falls in under two weeks and is one of the busiest travel periods in the US.

Aviation safety analyst Todd Curtis told Al Jazeera that recovery can take more than a day even after a technical fault is fixed, because aircraft and crews must be rescheduled across the whole network.

Travellers are being told to check flight status directly with their airline. Official updates on airport conditions are posted by the <a href="https://www.faa.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Federal Aviation Administration</a>.

## Why Ageing Air Traffic Systems Are Back in Focus

The outage has revived a long debate about the state of US air traffic control technology. Tony Stanton, a former senior executive at Australia's Civil Aviation Safety Authority, pointed to a September report by the US Government Accountability Office that warned about copper cabling dating back to the 1960s and poorly scheduled system changes. He also criticised a lack of contingency planning. Ron Bartsch of the University of the South Pacific questioned why backup systems did not take over immediately.

Duffy, who leads the <a href="https://www.transportation.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">US Department of Transportation</a>, has used the episode to call for $50 billion in air traffic control upgrades.

## What It Means for Indian Travellers and Students

Indian students, workers and tourists heading to the US often take connecting domestic flights after landing at a major American hub, so delays inside the US network can affect their trips too. Anyone with US travel planned over the next few days should:

- Check the latest flight status with the airline before leaving for the airport.
- Allow extra time for domestic connections, as knock-on delays may continue.
- Keep booking confirmations handy in case of rebooking or hotel claims with the airline.

## A Fragile System Under Scrutiny

Friday's halt did not put any aircraft in danger, according to US officials, but it showed how a fault in one part of an old network can freeze air travel across the world's largest aviation market within minutes. The final findings of the FBI and FAA investigation, and whether Congress acts on funding for new equipment, will decide how likely a repeat is.
