The United States sold its first offshore lease for seabed minerals on November 19, 2026, handing about 31.5 million acres of the Pacific floor off American Samoa to a US company for $6 million. The 20 minute live auction, run by the Interior Department's Marine Minerals Administration (MMA), went ahead three days after a federal court in Hawaii ruled against a key environmental approval behind it. India has been exploring the Indian Ocean floor since 2002 under an international licence, and it still cannot mine there.

| Key fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Sale date | November 19, 2026 (lease sale PACM-1) |
| Area | About 31.5 million acres (12.7 million hectares) in two lots |
| Winning bidder | Wetstone U.S. Ventures, the only company to bid |
| Price | $3 million per lot, $6 million total, about 19 US cents an acre |
| What the lease allows | Surveys and data collection only, for now |
| Next US sale | Northern Mariana Islands, about 67 million acres, December 16 |

## What Happened at the Auction

According to <a href="https://news.mongabay.com/2026/11/us-leases-american-samoan-seabed-for-mining-in-auction-deemed-illegal-by-court/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Mongabay's report from the sale</a>, two companies registered: Wetstone U.S. Ventures and the Norwegian firm Adepth. Only Wetstone bid, and it took both lots, one of about 15 million acres and one of about 16 million acres, at the opening price of $3 million each. The MMA, which absorbed the old Bureau of Ocean Energy Management's minerals work, said on its <a href="https://www.boem.gov/american-samoa" target="_blank" rel="noopener">American Samoa page</a> that the auction produced $6 million in high bids.

MMA official Douglas Boren said the leases cover only early surveys and data gathering. Any test mining or commercial extraction would need further Interior Department approval and a fresh environmental review. Acting MMA Director Michael Olsen called the sale "another step in securing American self-reliance."

The government named the lease area in November 2025, issued a proposed notice in July 2026 and a final notice on October 19, 2026, setting the sale date a month later.

## The Court Ruling the Sale Ignored

On November 16, a federal court in Hawaii ruled for community and conservation groups that had sued the National Marine Fisheries Service. The court found the review behind the sale had not properly weighed the effects on endangered species. Interior went ahead regardless.

Earthjustice, the lawyers for the groups, has a second motion pending that challenges the MMA's legal authority to hold the sale at all. If it succeeds, it could cancel the auction and the leases. Earthjustice attorney Chris Eaton called the department's decision "lawless."

American Samoa's own government cannot block the leases. Its 2024 ban on deep-sea mining covers waters up to 3 nautical miles from shore. Beyond that, out to 200 nautical miles, the federal government controls the seabed. Local opponents say the sale threatens the Rose Atoll Marine National Monument and the island's fishing and food security. NRDC said more than 76,000 public comments were filed, most of them opposed.

## What Is Under the Seabed

The targets are polymetallic nodules lying on the deep ocean floor and ferromanganese crusts, according to NRDC. India's own survey data shows what such nodules can hold: copper, nickel and cobalt (see the table below). The US government describes them as critical minerals for national security. Mongabay links the push to an April 2025 executive order from President Donald Trump.

No country has yet mined the deep seabed on a commercial scale. More than 45 countries have called for a ban or a pause until the science on deep ocean ecosystems is better understood.

## How India's Deep-Sea Route Differs

The US is leasing seabed inside its own exclusive economic zone under its own law. Most deep-sea mineral fields, though, lie in international waters, where exploration rights come from the International Seabed Authority (ISA). India works through that system.

| | US off American Samoa | India in the Indian Ocean |
|---|---|---|
| Who grants rights | US Interior Department (MMA) | International Seabed Authority |
| Where | US waters, 3 to 200 nautical miles offshore | International seabed |
| Area | About 31.5 million acres (roughly 127,000 sq km) | 75,000 sq km for nodules, 10,000 sq km for sulphides |
| Since | Lease sold November 2026 | Nodule contract signed March 2002, sulphide contract 2016 |
| Holder | Private company (Wetstone U.S. Ventures) | Government of India, through the Ministry of Earth Sciences |
| Commercial mining allowed? | Not yet, needs more approvals | No, ISA has not adopted mining rules |

India signed its first ISA contract on March 25, 2002 for 15 years. It has since been extended twice by five years, most recently through an agreement signed in February 2023, according to the <a href="https://isa.org.jm/news/the-ministry-of-earth-sciences-of-india-signs-a-second-extension-agreement-for-the-exploration-of-polymetallic-nodules-in-the-indian-ocean-with-the-international-seabed-authority/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ISA</a>. The Ministry of Earth Sciences told the Rajya Sabha in August 2025 that India's nodule area in the Central Indian Ocean Basin holds an estimated 366 million tonnes of nodules, with about 1.09 percent copper, 1.14 percent nickel and 0.14 percent cobalt. The ministry also said that deep-sea mining is not yet allowed, because the ISA has not finalised its exploitation regulations.

So India and other ISA contractors are waiting on a global rulebook, while the US is moving on its own inside its waters and, per Mongabay, is also pursuing claims in international areas such as the Clarion-Clipperton Zone in the Pacific.

## What to Watch Next

The next US sale, off the Northern Mariana Islands, is set for December 16 and covers about 67 million acres. Guam, Alaska and Virginia have been named as possible later sites. The Earthjustice motion in Hawaii will decide whether the American Samoa leases survive. Meanwhile, ISA talks on mining rules continue, and India's exploration contracts depend on their outcome.
