Italy's data protection authority, the Garante per la protezione dei dati personali, has fined Meta €805 million (about $887 million) over the way the Facebook app on Android phones reached into users' photo galleries. The decision, announced on Saturday, November 14, 2026, says the app read photos, videos and their metadata on the phones of Italian users without specific consent and without properly telling them. It is the largest penalty the Italian regulator has ever imposed.

The fine was issued to Meta Platforms Ireland Limited, the company's main European arm. The regulator estimates that about 20 million Android users in Italy could have been affected.

## The Facebook Feature at the Centre of the Case

The case is about a tool called "Get suggestions from gallery when browsing Facebook". Available on the Android app since 2023, it shows people photos and videos from their own phone while they scroll, suggesting them as ideas for new posts.

According to the Garante, the problem was not the idea itself but how permission was collected:

- One broad, app-wide permission covered gallery suggestions along with other media functions.
- The on-screen notice asked users to "enable other features" in generic terms, so people could not tell they were also switching on gallery suggestions.
- The feature was turned on by default.
- Once full access was granted, the app could read the entire photo archive, usually on a permanent basis.

The regulator said Meta changed the consent flow after a complaint was filed, but judged the change to be late and limited to people who update the app. In its view, the violation continues for users who have not updated and for those who had already granted the system permission.

## Why Italy Acted Under the ePrivacy Law, Not GDPR

Most large European privacy fines against Big Tech come under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), where the Irish Data Protection Commission usually takes the lead because many US firms have their EU base in Dublin. This case is different.

The Garante relied on Article 122 of the Italian Privacy Code, which brings the EU ePrivacy Directive into national law. Under that rule, the regulator argues, a smartphone is part of a person's private sphere, and reading what is stored on it needs free and informed consent. That applies whether or not the data leaves the device and whether or not any harm is shown.

Meta asked the Italian authority to hand the matter to Ireland. The Garante refused, saying the ePrivacy rules have no "one stop shop" system that would send the case to a single lead regulator. That point matters because a Rome court in March 2026 cancelled a €15 million GDPR fine the Garante had imposed on OpenAI, on the grounds that Ireland had become OpenAI's lead authority.

## What Meta Must Change and By When

Along with the fine, the regulator has given Meta a set of orders with deadlines. As reported by <a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/14/italy-fines-meta-880m-over-photo-privacy" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Al Jazeera</a> and other outlets, Meta must fix the unclear consent notice and stop the feature from being on by default.

| Deadline | What Meta must do |
|---|---|
| 30 days | Report the steps it has started |
| 60 days | Ask for a separate, specific consent that explains the feature, the gallery access and how to switch it off; keep it off by default, including for users who earlier gave permission |
| After 60 days | If not compliant, the feature cannot be switched on for users without valid consent |
| 90 days | Report the measures it has adopted |

The regulator also kept the right to look separately at a feature launched in 2026 that processes gallery content in the cloud.

## How the €805 Million Figure Was Reached

The Garante said the fine works out to roughly 0.47% of the group's worldwide turnover. It listed several factors that pushed the amount higher:

- The large number of people affected, about 20 million
- The fact that the violation is still going on for some users
- An earlier 2019 measure against the company
- What it called Meta's "lack of cooperation"

The changes Meta made after the complaint were counted in its favour, though the regulator described them as late and limited. The complaint itself was filed in July 2025 by the Centro Tutela Consumatori Utenti, a consumer group based in Bolzano, and the regulator also ran its own technical tests.

The fine is large by Italian standards but smaller than the €1.2 billion that Ireland's regulator imposed on Meta in May 2023 over transfers of European user data to the United States.

## AI Training Question Left Open

The Garante made clear that its investigation did not examine whether the gallery content was used to train artificial intelligence systems. Meta denies any such use.

As of Saturday, Meta had not publicly commented. <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-14/italy-fines-meta-805-million-over-access-to-users-photos" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Bloomberg reported</a> that the company did not immediately respond to a request for comment. Meta has the right to appeal, as OpenAI did in its own case before the Italian courts.

## What the Ruling Means for Smartphone Users Outside Italy

The decision applies only in Italy, but it sends a signal about how European regulators view apps that read the contents of a phone. The key message is that a single, vaguely worded permission screen is not enough when an app wants access to something as personal as a photo library.

For Android users anywhere, including in India, the case is a reminder to look at which apps have full access to photos and videos in the phone's privacy or permissions settings, and to limit that access to selected items where the option exists. ## What Happens Next for Meta in Europe

Meta now has 30 days to report its first steps to the Garante and 60 days to roll out a clearer consent request in Italy. Whether it appeals, and whether other European regulators take a similar view under their own ePrivacy laws, will shape how much this case changes the way apps ask for gallery access across the region.
