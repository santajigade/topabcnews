Astronomers using early images from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile have found two objects orbiting our galaxy that had never been seen before. One is Aquarius IV, an extremely faint dwarf galaxy on the far edges of the Milky Way. The other is Rubin-GC1, a star cluster hidden behind thick clouds of cosmic dust. Both were spotted in preview data recorded before the telescope's main survey even began, and researchers say they are likely the first of many such finds.

The results were announced by Yale University and the University of Washington in late October and early November, and are described in two scientific papers. They matter because small, dim companions like these are some of the best natural laboratories for studying dark matter, the invisible material that makes up most of the mass in the universe.

## Aquarius IV: A Tiny Galaxy at the Galaxy's Edge

Aquarius IV is an ultra-faint dwarf galaxy, a class of galaxy that holds very few stars but a great deal of dark matter. The Yale release places it about 355,000 light-years from Earth, deep in the outer halo of the Milky Way. It is so dim that it is barely visible by eye in the survey images, but automated search software picked it out with high confidence.

According to Live Science, the galaxy has an absolute magnitude of about minus 1.9, making it roughly 14 million times dimmer than the Milky Way. One researcher estimated its mass at around 1,500 times that of the Sun. The detection was confirmed in older images from the Dark Energy Camera. The discovery paper, led by Yale graduate student William Cerny, appeared in <a href="https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/2515-5172/ae993b" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Research Notes of the American Astronomical Society</a>.

## Rubin-GC1: A Star Cluster Hidden Behind Dust

The second object lies on the opposite side of the galaxy, in a region so thick with dust that earlier surveys missed it. Unlike Aquarius IV, Rubin-GC1 stands out clearly in Rubin's images as a crowd of individual stars once you know where to look.

The paper describing it, led by Aashay Pai of the University of Chicago and accepted by The Astrophysical Journal Letters, calls it a globular cluster in the direction of the constellation Ophiuchus. The team estimates it is about 31 kiloparsecs away, or roughly 100,000 light-years, and about 9.2 billion years old. The authors describe it as Rubin's first globular cluster discovery. More than 140 globular clusters were already known in the Milky Way.

## How Rubin Found Them Before Its Survey Began

The finds came from observations taken between April 2025 and January 2026, released as Data Preview 2. These images covered only about 7 percent of the night sky, or roughly 15 percent of the area the full survey will eventually map, according to the University of Washington.

Rubin sits on Cerro Pachon in the Chilean Andes and uses the 8.4 metre Simonyi Survey Telescope with a 3.2 gigapixel camera, the largest digital camera ever built. Its 10 year Legacy Survey of Space and Time began in June and will repeatedly photograph the southern sky. The observatory is jointly funded by the US <a href="https://www.nsf.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">National Science Foundation</a> and the US Department of Energy's Office of Science.

More than 20 University of Washington researchers worked on cleaning and checking the star catalogue. UW postdoctoral scholar Peter Ferguson, a co-author on both studies, explained that even a few misclassified galaxies or false detections can drown out the faint signal of an object like Aquarius IV.

## Why Faint Satellites Matter for Dark Matter Research

About 65 satellite galaxies of the Milky Way are known today, but some estimates suggest hundreds more remain undiscovered. Ultra-faint dwarfs are dominated by dark matter, so counting them and measuring their properties lets scientists test competing theories of how dark matter behaves and how galaxies like ours were assembled over billions of years.

"These discoveries are just the beginning for Rubin," Cerny said. Yale astronomer Marla Geha, a co-author of both papers, said further finds will help complete the census of satellite galaxies around the Milky Way.

## What to Expect From Rubin in the Coming Years

If two new companions turned up in a small slice of preview data, the full survey is expected to find many more. Live Science reported that Rubin could identify up to 100 ultra-faint dwarf galaxies over the course of the survey. Each new find will add a piece to the map of the Milky Way's halo, and to the long search for what dark matter actually is.
