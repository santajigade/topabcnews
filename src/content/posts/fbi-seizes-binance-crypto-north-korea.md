The United States has seized more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange, in a theft the FBI blames on North Korean military hackers. The US Justice Department announced the action on Thursday, November 12, saying government and private sector efforts have frozen more than $2.7 billion of the stolen funds so far. At the same time, officials revealed they had taken down the online infrastructure behind a separate North Korean scam, called FaceStealer, that used fake video calls to drain more than $400 million from crypto users over 18 months.

## How Much Was Stolen From Binance

According to the <a href="https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Justice Department's official announcement</a>, on or about October 29 members of the North Korean military stole more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance. CBS News put the total at about $6.3 billion and described it as the biggest crypto heist in history. The FBI tracks the group responsible under the name "TraderTraitor."

Investigators say the work is not finished. The hackers are still moving the remaining money through crypto bridges, mixers and exchanges to hide its trail, and agencies are continuing to trace and freeze it. Last week, the FBI, the US Secret Service and the IRS Criminal Investigation unit issued a Cyber Alert attributing the theft to North Korea and asking crypto service providers to block transactions linked to the laundering addresses.

## What US Officials Said

Assistant Attorney General A. Tysen Duva, who heads the Justice Department's Criminal Division, said the quick freezing of funds had stopped "about half of this historic breach." M. Scott Proctor of the department's National Security Division said North Korea's government funds its regime and weapons programme through crypto theft.

Bill Essayli, First Assistant US Attorney for the Central District of California, said crypto "has become fertile ground for North Korea to defraud victims," according to CBS News. US Attorney for the District of Columbia Jeanine Pirro also highlighted the size of the seizure.

CBS News noted that in cases like this, the government usually files a forfeiture claim in court, and a judge then decides who has a valid claim to the recovered funds. None of the reports reviewed for this article included a statement from Binance.

## Inside the FaceStealer Video Call Scam

The second operation shows how North Korean hackers use trust as a weapon. According to the Justice Department and an FBI alert described by CyberScoop, the scheme works in a few steps:

1. Hackers take over accounts on Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack and Discord.
2. From these real but hijacked accounts, they invite contacts to a video meeting.
3. The meeting link opens a look-alike website that copies the design of Zoom or Microsoft Teams.
4. While the victim waits, their webcam is secretly recorded. Attackers play prerecorded clips of known people, sometimes deepfakes of well-known crypto figures.
5. The attackers then fake a technical problem, such as claiming the victim cannot be heard, and ask them to install a "fix" that is actually malware.

Once inside, the hackers steal crypto, take over accounts months or even more than a year later, and mine the victim's contacts for new targets. Under a federal warrant, the FBI seized more than 900 webcam videos of victims from the infrastructure. CyberScoop reported that earlier this year the same tactic was used to trick the maintainer of a widely used open-source JavaScript library into a fake Teams meeting, giving attackers a route to plant malicious code in software that others download.

The FBI has published guidance and steps for victims on its <a href="https://www.fbi.gov/facestealer" target="_blank" rel="noopener">FaceStealer information page</a>. The investigation was led by the FBI's Los Angeles Field Office, with help from security firms Arctic Wolf and Jumpsec and Microsoft's threat intelligence team.

## Lessons for Crypto Users and Developers in India

FaceStealer targets crypto and Web3 workers and open-source developers, groups that are spread around the world, including in India's large developer community. The warning signs are simple to remember:

- Be suspicious of a sudden video call invite, even from a known contact, especially if it asks you to install anything.
- Check the web address of any meeting link carefully. Fake sites can look exactly like real ones.
- Never install a "patch" or "audio fix" sent during a call. Use only the official app store or vendor site.
- Confirm unusual requests through a second channel, such as a phone call.

Video meeting tools have been in the news for other reasons this week too, with Teams among the services hit in a large outage, as covered in our report on the <a href="/microsoft-outlook-teams-outage-november-12/">Microsoft Outlook and Teams disruption</a>.

## A Rare Win Against State Crypto Hackers

Recovering stolen crypto is usually difficult once it has been laundered across many wallets. Freezing roughly $2.7 billion within about two weeks of the Binance theft is a significant result for US agencies and their private partners. Still, more than half of the stolen money remains out of reach, and Justin Moore of security firm Arctic Wolf told CyberScoop that North Korean schemes have grown significantly in scale and sophistication. The coming weeks will show whether more of the Binance funds can be traced before they disappear for good.
