Countries meeting in Geneva under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) agreed early on Saturday, November 21, 2026, to start work on an international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons systems, with 2028 as the target. They did not agree to negotiate a binding treaty, and they set no ban. The CCW's Seventh Review Conference ran from November 16 to 20 and went past schedule into Saturday morning, according to AFP and Gizmodo.

The CCW has 128 states parties and works by consensus, so one country can block any decision.

## What the Geneva Text Actually Commits Countries To

Conference president Julien Thoni announced that states had agreed to "launch the elaboration of an international instrument" on these weapons, AFP reported. He said the process should sort out which weapons can be banned under international humanitarian law and which can be allowed with regulation.

Al Jazeera reported that some states rejected the word "negotiate", so the text says "elaboration" instead. The agreement creates no binding commitments, and it does not say whether the final instrument will be a treaty. Activist Beatrice Fihn described it on X as text watered down to "elaborate" on "an instrument", Gizmodo reported.

| Question | What was agreed |
|---|---|
| Treaty talks launched? | No. States agreed to "elaborate" an instrument |
| Deadline | 2028 |
| Ban on any weapon type? | None yet. Prohibited and regulated categories still to be decided |
| Binding commitments? | None created |
| Decision rule | Consensus among 128 states parties |

## Who Pushed and Who Held Back

A majority of states wanted a legally binding agreement, according to AP and AFP. Before the conference, seven countries, Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama, proposed opening negotiations on a rulebook, AP reported. That push fell short.

Al Jazeera reported that major arms producers, including the United States, Russia and Israel, opposed it. Diplomats told the outlet that Russian procedural objections led to some formal sessions being called off. AFP said Washington and Moscow want the freedom to keep developing such systems.

UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres had set 2026 as the deadline to launch negotiations. That deadline has now passed, and Guterres leaves office at the end of the year, AP noted. Izumi Nakamitsu, the UN's disarmament chief, called the outcome a significant achievement but also described it as a glass half full or half empty.

"Technology is moving faster than diplomacy," Brazil's ambassador Frederico Meyer told AP. The Stop Killer Robots coalition of about 300 groups called the mandate watered down. Its executive director Nicole van Rooijen told AFP that delay "puts civilians and humanity at risk".

## A Decade of Talks: How the Process Reached 2028

This timeline is built from Carnegie India's research and the reports above.

| Year | Step |
|---|---|
| 2016 | CCW sets up a Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on lethal autonomous weapons |
| 2017 to 2018 | India chairs the GGE while its guiding principles are drafted |
| 2019 | 11 guiding principles adopted |
| 2023 | GGE told to develop, by consensus, "a set of elements for an instrument" |
| December 2023 | UN General Assembly resolution 78/241 on these weapons passes; 4 states vote against |
| September 4, 2026 | GGE ends its mandate; final report endorsed by 76 members |
| November 21, 2026 | Review Conference agrees to elaborate an instrument by 2028 |

Carnegie India's November 12 analysis noted that the GGE's final report weakened the earlier draft's sections on the definition of these weapons, humanitarian law and human control.

## Where India Stands on Autonomous Weapons

India's most recent working paper to the GGE, dated May 16, 2025 and published by the <a href="https://fsi.mea.gov.in/Images/CPV/India_working_paper_GGE_LAWS.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ministry of External Affairs</a>, sets out these points:

- India supports prohibiting the use of fully autonomous weapons that cannot comply with international humanitarian law.
- Other autonomous weapons should be covered by regulation, not a ban, along with voluntary best practices and technical safeguards.
- Regulation should target how a system is used and its effects, not the underlying technology. The paper says "Technology is value-neutral".
- Responsibility for using such weapons always rests with the human operator.
- Systems already used with a human in or on the loop, and uncrewed vehicles, should be kept out of scope.

The paper does not say directly whether India backs a legally binding treaty. Carnegie India's 2024 study, citing Indian statements, said New Delhi saw a binding instrument as premature while there is no agreed definition. India preferred a political declaration built on the 2019 principles. The same study, citing UN records, said India was one of four countries, with Belarus, Mali and Russia, that voted against the 2023 General Assembly resolution, arguing it duplicated the GGE's work.

None of the reports from this week's Geneva meeting describe India's statements there. The aim Thoni set out for the 2028 process, banning some weapons and regulating the rest, matches the split India proposed in its 2025 paper. Whether the result will bind anyone is still undecided.

## Why the Delay Matters Now

AP reported that armed drones able to strike targets on their own have been deployed in some conflict zones, though not widely. Stop Killer Robots says weapons that use AI to select targets are already in combat use. Gizmodo, citing a New York Times report, described a Russian drone in Ukraine this summer that is believed to have acted autonomously.
