Today, Friday, November 13, 2026, is the date that a famous scientific paper published 66 years ago named as "Doomsday." In 1960, the journal Science carried a study by Austrian American physicist Heinz von Foerster and two colleagues titled "Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026." Their maths suggested that if human population kept growing the way it had for nearly 2,000 years, it would shoot towards infinity around this date. The world, of course, is still here. The story of why the forecast failed says a lot about how people, not just numbers, shape the future.

## What the 1960 Doomsday Paper Calculated

Von Foerster's team took estimates of world population going back about two millennia and fitted them to a mathematical growth curve. They noticed something striking: the time it took for the population to double kept getting shorter. Growth was not just fast, it was speeding up.

When the researchers extended that curve forward, it reached a mathematical "singularity", a point where the population would become infinitely large, in November 2026. In the authors' words, people would not starve or run out of energy first. Instead, as Newsweek noted, the paper warned: "They will be squeezed to death."

The <a href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.132.3436.1291" target="_blank" rel="noopener">original paper in Science</a> was published in November 1960, when the world had roughly 3 billion people.

## Was the Date a Joke?

Partly, yes. Experts quoted by Newsweek say the exact day was never meant as a literal prophecy.

- Joel Cohen, a mathematical biologist at The Rockefeller University, said the Friday the 13th date was a joke, and that November 13 was von Foerster's own birthday.
- Cohen added that the fine print of the paper's key equation carried an error margin of plus or minus five and a half years.
- Alan MacEachern, a historian at Western University in Canada, said he did not think the authors "genuinely believed" humanity would end on that day.

Euronews reported that the eye-catching date was chosen to draw attention to overpopulation, which was a hotly debated issue at the time. Death rates had fallen while birth rates stayed high, and fears grew further after the 1968 book The Population Bomb by Paul and Anne Ehrlich.

## Where the Forecast Worked, and Where It Broke Down

For a while, the curve tracked real numbers well. Euronews said it matched actual population figures until the early 1990s, and MacEachern told Newsweek it gave fairly accurate projections from the 1960s to the 1980s before starting to falter around 1993.

The key flaw was an assumption that people would keep behaving as they always had. Cohen said von Foerster "didn't allow for the possibility that people might choose to have fewer children." That is what happened: Euronews noted that population growth slowed significantly after the early 1990s.

The population has kept rising, but far more slowly than the 1960 curve demanded. According to <a href="https://www.un.org/en/global-issues/population" target="_blank" rel="noopener">United Nations population estimates</a>, as summarised by Euronews, the global total is expected to peak at around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s and then slowly decline.

## From Overpopulation Fears to Ageing Societies

The worry in many countries today is almost the reverse of 1960. Newsweek noted that governments are increasingly concerned about low birth rates, ageing populations and shrinking workforces, pointing to recent Federal Reserve analysis of the shift.

Euronews writer Patrick Drews argued that forecasts like von Foerster's go wrong because they simply extend past trends and ignore how people adapt to problems. He compared it with the 1972 study The Limits to Growth, which also projected trends forward, only for new resources to be found and some raw materials to become cheaper.

## Why Old Predictions Still Matter

The doomsday paper is often cited as a warning about forecasting: a model can fit past data almost perfectly and still fail badly when the conditions behind it change. That lesson applies well beyond population, to climate, economics and technology forecasts. Real long term challenges remain, from resource use to emissions, as our report on <a href="/global-carbon-budget-2026-fossil-emissions-fall/">the Global Carbon Budget 2026</a> shows, but they are shaped by human choices that no simple curve can capture.

## Key Highlights

- A 1960 Science paper by Heinz von Foerster named Friday, November 13, 2026 as the date population growth would hit infinity.
- The date was partly a joke, linked to von Foerster's birthday, and the equation carried an error margin of about five and a half years.
- The model tracked real data until about the early 1990s, then failed as birth rates fell.
- World population has grown from about 3 billion to over 8 billion, and the UN expects a peak near 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s.

## A Doomsday That Never Came

Sixty six years later, the "end of the world" date has arrived without incident. Rather than proving the scientists foolish, it shows that the 1960 paper did its job of starting a debate. Humanity changed course on its own, and the curve that once pointed to doom now bends towards a population peak later this century.
