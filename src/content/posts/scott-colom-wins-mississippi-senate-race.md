Democrat Scott Colom has won the US Senate race in Mississippi, defeating Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith in one of the closest contests of the 2026 midterm elections. The Associated Press called the race on Wednesday, November 11, more than a week after polling day on November 3, once counties finished reviewing absentee and provisional ballots. The result is the eighth Republican-held Senate seat that Democrats have flipped this cycle, and it makes Colom the first Black senator elected in Mississippi since 1874.

## A Count That Took More Than a Week

The race was too close to call for days. CBS News reported that the morning after the election, with about 96% of the vote counted, only a few dozen votes separated the two candidates. Colom's lead then grew slowly as officials worked through the remaining ballots.

Much of the delay came from affidavit ballots, the name Mississippi uses for provisional ballots, which county officials must review one by one. According to TIME, Colom had 49.2% of the vote and Hyde-Smith 48.7% with about 99% of ballots counted, a gap of roughly half a percentage point.

Mississippi law does not provide for automatic recounts or recounts requested by candidates, though a court can order one. In the end that question did not arise. Hyde-Smith conceded in a short video message, saying the result was "not the outcome we worked for or hoped for" and that she respected the voters' decision. A spokesperson said she had called Colom to concede before posting the video.

## Who Is Scott Colom

Colom, 43, is the district attorney for Mississippi's 16th Judicial District and describes himself as a seventh-generation Mississippian. In 2022, then President Joe Biden nominated him to a federal judgeship in the Northern District of Mississippi. Hyde-Smith blocked that nomination using the Senate custom that allows home-state senators to hold up judicial picks, which gave this year's contest a personal edge.

Hyde-Smith, 67, was appointed to the Senate in 2018 after Thad Cochran resigned. She then won a special election that year and a full term in 2020, beating Democrat Mike Espy both times. She was the first woman elected to represent Mississippi in Congress.

At a victory rally at Tougaloo College, Colom said he made "a solemn commitment to each and every Mississippian." He also told supporters that there were times when donors would not take his calls, but that he "kept the faith."

## What Decided the Race

Mississippi is one of the most reliably Republican states in the country. Donald Trump carried it by nearly 23 points in 2024, according to AP and TIME, and the last time the state elected a Democrat to the Senate was 1982.

Reports from the AP, CBS and TIME point to several factors behind the upset:

- **Cost of living:** Colom built his campaign around prices and healthcare. He branded his rival "Cindy High Prices" after she remarked that shoppers had many proteins to choose from when asked about high beef prices.
- **Turnout:** The AP said strong turnout among Black voters helped Colom.
- **The Iran war:** Colom opposed the war, arguing it had raised costs for families. Hyde-Smith accepted its economic impact but called it necessary for national security.
- **Healthcare and spending:** Hyde-Smith backed Trump's large tax and spending bill and its Medicaid cuts, which Colom said he wants to help reverse.
- **No debates:** Colom criticised Hyde-Smith for refusing to debate him.

Hyde-Smith's campaign, in turn, described Colom as "the most radical liberal" and attacked his positions on abortion and transgender issues. Colom calls himself pro-life but says women should make their own choices. Bill Clinton and Kamala Harris campaigned for him in the final weeks, and national Democrats began spending on ads in the state only last month.

## What It Means for Control of the US Senate

With Mississippi, Democrats have now flipped Republican seats in North Carolina, South Carolina, Texas, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas and Maine, according to CBS News. When the new Congress meets in January, Democrats and the independents who caucus with them will hold at least 55 of the 100 seats, against 44 for Republicans. The race in Alaska is still undecided.

That is a clear majority, but still short of the 60 votes usually needed to overcome a filibuster on most legislation. The majority party sets the chamber's agenda and controls its committees, including those that hold hearings on presidential nominees. The official <a href="https://www.senate.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">US Senate website</a> will list the new membership once results are certified.
