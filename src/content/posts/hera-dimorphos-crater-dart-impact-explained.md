When NASA flew its DART spacecraft into a small asteroid in 2022, scientists could measure how much the asteroid's orbit changed, but they could not see what the crash did to the rock itself. The European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft has now answered that. Images released on November 25, 2026 show a crater on Dimorphos about the size of a stadium, covering nearly the whole side that DART hit. The asteroid is also tumbling, and hundreds of boulders are drifting around the pair.

Neither Dimorphos nor its larger partner Didymos poses any threat to Earth. The pair was chosen as a safe place to test whether a spacecraft can push an asteroid off course.

## The Numbers Behind the Test

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| DART impact date | September 26, 2022 |
| DART mass | 580 kg |
| Impact speed | 6.1 km per second (about 22,530 km/h) |
| Dimorphos size | About 151 m across |
| Didymos size | About 730 m across |
| Gap between the two | About 1 km |
| Change in Dimorphos's orbit | About 33 minutes |
| Debris plume after impact | Spanned 10,000 km |
| Hera launch | October 7, 2024 |
| Hera's current distance from the pair | About 25 to 30 km |

Dimorphos is a moonlet that circles Didymos. DART's hit slowed it down and pulled its orbit in, so each lap now takes about 33 minutes less, according to ESA. BBC Sky at Night Magazine put the change at around 32 minutes. Either way, it was the first time people had moved a natural body in the Solar System by a measurable amount.

## What the New Images Show

The Planetary Society described four of the first close-up views. One shows the crater on Dimorphos. Another sets DART's image of the asteroid beside Hera's view from four years later. A third catches Dimorphos casting its shadow on Didymos. The fourth is a set of long exposures with the asteroids' own light removed, which reveals dotted trails of boulders orbiting the system.

Michael Küppers, ESA's Hera mission scientist, told Reuters: "Dimorphos is now tumbling in a chaotic way." He added that the team had not known what happened to Dimorphos itself, only that DART had hit it. He also said Hera saw no evidence of major changes on the side opposite the impact.

Scientists running computer models, including astrophysicist and Queen guitarist Brian May, believe Dimorphos has been completely reshaped, Reuters reported. Hera's mission manager unveiled the images in the dinosaur hall of the Museum für Naturkunde in Berlin, a nod to the asteroid impact about 66 million years ago that wiped out the dinosaurs.

Hera's principal investigator Patrick Michel said of the first look: "The first thing we said was wow!"

## Before and After: What DART Told Us and What Hera Adds

DART's job in 2022 was to show that a spacecraft can change an asteroid's path. Telescopes on Earth confirmed that by timing Dimorphos's orbit. What they could not do was see the asteroid up close. Hera was built to go back and inspect the crash site.

| Question | Known after DART (2022) | Seen by Hera (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Did the orbit change? | Yes, by about 33 minutes per lap | Hera will measure the system in detail over the coming months |
| What did the impact face look like? | Unknown | A crater covering nearly the whole front side |
| How is Dimorphos moving? | Unknown | Tumbling in a chaotic way |
| Where did the debris go? | A plume spanning 10,000 km | Hundreds of boulders still orbiting the pair |
| Was the far side changed? | Unknown | No evidence of major changes, Küppers said |

Küppers said the combination of the chaotic tumble and the hundreds of floating boulders was surprising.

## Flying Slowly Around a Pair of Rocks

Hera is roughly the size of a small car. Gravity near these asteroids is extremely weak: ESA says Didymos pulls about 40,000 times more weakly than Earth, and Dimorphos about 200,000 times more weakly. So Hera does not orbit in the usual sense.

According to <a href="https://www.esa.int/articles/hera-spacecraft-in-rectangular-orbit-around-target-asteroids" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ESA's operations update</a>, the spacecraft moves at about 12 cm per second around the pair's shared centre of gravity. It coasts for three days, then spends four days moving away, firing thrusters to turn 90 degrees each time. The result is a series of rectangular paths. If a thruster firing fails, Hera simply drifts away instead of hitting the asteroids. Flight controllers at ESOC in Darmstadt, Germany, talk to it for about 8 hours a day through 35 m deep space antennas in Argentina, Spain and Australia. On November 11, ESA put the spacecraft about 196.5 million km from Earth.

## What Hera Does Next: December and January

| When | Step |
|---|---|
| December 2026 | Detailed characterisation phase: bowtie-shaped arcs starting from about 20 km, then down to about 13 km, with sharper images |
| About six weeks after Nov 25 (January 2027) | Release of two shoebox-sized CubeSats, Milani and Juventas |
| Coming months | Measuring the DART crater and mapping Dimorphos's surface |
| End of mission | Final descent onto Didymos, timed by remaining propellant |

The two CubeSats will act like drones and take risks the main spacecraft cannot. Milani will study the minerals on Dimorphos's surface. Juventas will carry out the first radar sounding of the inside of an asteroid, which should show whether Dimorphos is solid rock or a loose pile of rubble. Both are the first ESA CubeSats to work beyond Earth orbit, and both will end by touching down on Dimorphos. The <a href="https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Hera/Hera_mission_overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Hera mission overview</a> says the main spacecraft will finish with its own descent onto Didymos.

## A Crash Site, Studied Four Years On

Ian Carnelli, Hera's mission manager, told Sky at Night Magazine the team sees this as only the start. ESA plans a close crash site investigation over the coming months: measuring the crater, mapping the surface, and sending the CubeSats to probe what lies beneath it. The <a href="https://www.planetary.org/articles/heras-first-close-up-images-of-didymos-and-dimorphos" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Planetary Society has the first image set</a> for anyone who wants to compare the before and after views.
