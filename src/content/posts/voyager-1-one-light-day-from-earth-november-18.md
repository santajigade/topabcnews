NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft will become the first human-made object to reach a full light-day from Earth on Wednesday, November 18, 2026. At that point the probe will be about 25.9 billion kilometres away, so far that a radio message sent from Earth will take 24 hours to reach it, and a reply will take another 24 hours to come back. The milestone arrives 49 years after launch, with the ageing probe still sending data from interstellar space.

## When Voyager 1 Crosses the One Light-Day Mark

According to <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-1/voyager-1-what-is-a-light-day/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NASA's Voyager mission page</a>, the crossing will happen at 5:16:07 a.m. Eastern Time on November 18. That is 10:16 a.m. UTC, or about 3:46 p.m. in India. NASA notes the exact moment could shift slightly because both the spacecraft and Earth are always moving.

NASA describes the event as a mile marker reached by no other human-made object. By then Voyager 1 will have spent 49 years, two months and 11 days travelling to that distance since its launch on September 5, 1977.

### Key highlights

- **Date:** Wednesday, November 18, 2026, 5:16:07 a.m. EST (about 3:46 p.m. IST)
- **Distance:** 16,094,799,096 miles, or 25,902,068,356 km
- **One-way signal time:** 24 hours
- **Round-trip signal time:** about 48 hours
- **Speed:** nearly 61,000 km per hour, according to NASA
- **Launched:** September 5, 1977
- **Entered interstellar space:** August 2012

## What a Light-Day Actually Means

A light-year is the distance light covers in a year. A light-day is the same idea over 24 hours. Light is the fastest thing in the universe, so even this small slice of a light-year is enormous by human standards.

NASA offers some comparisons to make the scale easier to picture:

| Object | Distance from Earth in light travel time |
|---|---|
| The Moon | About 1.3 seconds |
| The Sun | About 8 minutes 20 seconds |
| Neptune | About 4 hours 10 minutes |
| Voyager 1 on Nov 18, 2026 | 24 hours |

In practical terms, this changes how engineers talk to the spacecraft. NASA's visualisation team said that if the mission team sends a command at the moment of the milestone, they would get the reply at the same time on November 19. Any problem on board takes at least two days to diagnose and respond to.

## Why Voyager 1 Matters After 49 Years

Voyager 1 holds two records at once: it is the most distant human-made object and the first to enter interstellar space, the region beyond the bubble of charged particles blown out by the Sun.

Along the way it changed what scientists knew about the outer planets. NASA lists several of its achievements:

- It found active volcanoes on Jupiter's moon Io, the first eruptions seen on another world.
- It showed that Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a giant, rotating, high-pressure storm.
- It measured Saturn's atmosphere and revealed that the planet's rings are not smooth, uniform sheets.
- It took the first "family portrait" of the solar system and the famous "Pale Blue Dot" image of Earth from beyond Neptune's orbit in 1990.

Both Voyager probes also carry a Golden Record, a message from humanity meant to drift through the Milky Way long after the spacecraft fall silent.

## How Long Can the Old Probe Keep Going

Voyager 1 is still working, but it is running on limited power. NASA says many of its science instruments have been switched off to save energy, including its cameras. IFLScience reported that the team turned off another instrument a few months ago, and that engineers are planning further steps to extend the life of both Voyagers.

The twin probe, Voyager 2, launched about two weeks earlier on August 20, 1977, but took a slower route to Jupiter. It entered interstellar space in December 2018 and is on pace to reach one light-day from Earth in November 2035, according to NASA.

Voyager 1 is headed in the general direction of a star called AC +79 3888, also known as Gliese 445. NASA says it will pass within about 1.7 light-years of that star in roughly 40,000 years.

## NASA's "Shine Bright" Invitation to the Public

To mark the milestone, NASA has invited people around the world to take part in a campaign called "Shine Bright for Voyager's Light-Day". The idea is simple: after sunset on November 17, people are asked to point a light, such as a torch or phone flashlight, towards the night sky as a symbolic signal to the spacecraft.

For readers in India, the event falls on the evening of November 17 local time. NASA's video team said it would post interview clips and background footage about the milestone on November 16, with live interviews with mission experts on November 17.

## A Distance No Spacecraft Has Reached Before

The one light-day crossing does not change Voyager 1's path or its science, but it gives a simple way to grasp how far a machine built in the 1970s has travelled. A probe launched in 1977 is now so far away that a single "hello" takes a full day to arrive. As long as its power holds, it will keep adding to that record every second, with Voyager 2 set to follow it across the same line nine years from now.
