The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) has successfully ground tested a redesigned third stage of its Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV), a key step towards bringing its most trusted rocket back into regular service. The test took place on Thursday, November 12, 2026, at the Static Test Facility of the Satish Dhawan Space Centre in Sriharikota, Andhra Pradesh. It follows two PSLV missions, PSLV-C61 and PSLV-C62, in which the same third stage, known as PS3, showed anomalies that cost ISRO both satellites.

## Why the PS3 Stage Needed a Redesign

The PSLV is a multi-stage rocket, and the PS3 is a solid motor stage that fires after the first two stages have done their work. According to The Tribune and Deccan Chronicle, anomalies were observed in this stage during two back-to-back missions:

| Mission | Satellite | When | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| PSLV-C61 | EOS-09 | May 2025 | Third stage anomaly, mission failed |
| PSLV-C62 | EOS-N1 | January 2026 | Third stage anomaly, mission failed |

EOS-09, also called RISAT-1B, was a radar imaging satellite designed to see the Earth day or night and in any weather. Background reports on the PSLV-C61 mission, launched on May 18, 2025, linked that failure to a drop in chamber pressure in the third stage during its burn. ISRO has not published the full failure analysis reports for either mission, and the latest statement does not describe the root cause.

A second failure of the same stage within months was a serious setback for a rocket that has carried Earth observation satellites, navigation spacecraft and commercial payloads for many years.

## What ISRO Changed in the New Third Stage

After the two failures, ISRO carried out a detailed review of the PS3 design. The review covered design margins, the rules for accepting raw materials, assembly steps and quality checks. Based on the reports, the main changes are:

- **Nozzle:** The nozzle configuration of the solid motor has been improved to give more design margin.
- **Mechanical interfaces:** The joints and connection points have been strengthened.
- **Seals:** Backup, or redundancy, has been added to the sealing systems.
- **Raw materials:** Stricter acceptance criteria now apply to materials used in the motor.
- **Assembly and inspection:** Assembly procedures have been updated, and non-destructive testing (NDT) has been revised for different stages of integration and transport.

In its statement, ISRO said "the new version of the PS3 Solid motor stage is realised and tested successfully." It added that the mechanical interfaces have been made stronger with redundancy in the sealing systems.

## What a Static Test Shows

In a static test, the rocket motor is fixed firmly to a test stand on the ground and fired as it would be during a real flight, without leaving the stand. Engineers record data such as pressure, thrust and temperature throughout the burn and compare it with what the design predicts. A clean static fire gives confidence that changes to hardware like nozzles and seals behave as expected before the stage is trusted with a costly satellite.

Deccan Chronicle reported that ISRO will carry out a detailed analysis and review of the test data before taking up further tests. The CSR Journal said more tests are planned to validate the changes before the PSLV resumes regular launches. ISRO has not announced a date for the PSLV's return to flight.

## What It Means for India's Space Plans

The PSLV has long been a workhorse for India, carrying national satellites as well as commercial payloads. A pause in its flights slows Earth observation programmes of the kind EOS-09 was meant to serve, such as disaster response and surveillance, and can hold up commercial launches.

ISRO's other rockets have kept flying in the meantime. In late October, ISRO launched the NVS-03 navigation satellite on a GSLV rocket, according to The CSR Journal, which also mentioned plans for upcoming missions including Gaganyaan. ## Key Highlights

- ISRO ground tested an upgraded PSLV third stage (PS3) at Sriharikota on November 12, 2026.
- The same stage showed anomalies in the failed PSLV-C61 (EOS-09) and PSLV-C62 (EOS-N1) missions.
- Changes include an improved nozzle, stronger mechanical interfaces, redundant seals and stricter material and inspection rules.
- Test data will be analysed before more tests, and no return to flight date has been set.

## The Road Back to Flight for PSLV

One successful ground test does not mean the problem is fully solved, and ISRO has been careful to say more checks will follow. Still, the result is the clearest sign yet that the agency has identified fixes it trusts. If the follow-up tests go well, the next PSLV launch will be watched closely as the real proof that India's most reliable rocket is back on track.
