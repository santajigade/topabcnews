The World Health Organization (WHO) is now treating the health threat from the current El Nino as an emergency. The UN health agency has classified the expected health impacts of the weather pattern as a health emergency, the first time it has done so for a climate related event, according to the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). To back this up, WHO has put a Grade 3 emergency response into action through a new global plan published on November 2, 2026, with an initial funding need of about US$35 million.

## What WHO's El Nino Response Plan Includes

The document, called the WHO Global Preparedness and Response to El Nino Related Public Health Risks and Emergencies Strategic Preparedness and Response Plan, covers the period from October 2026 to September 2027. It activates WHO's Grade 3 response across its headquarters, regional offices and country offices, while supporting national governments to lead their own preparations.

Under WHO's grading system, a Grade 3 emergency calls for "major to maximal" support from the organisation, Gizmodo reported. The plan follows an "anticipate, mitigate, respond" approach, meaning countries are encouraged to act on forecasts and early warning triggers before disease outbreaks or heat deaths begin.

The plan is built around four strategic objectives:

- **Detection and monitoring:** climate informed disease surveillance so outbreaks are spotted early.
- **Protection and prevention:** shielding vulnerable groups and keeping essential health services running.
- **Care and support:** treating those affected by heat, floods, drought and disease.
- **Health leadership and coordination:** organising support for member states, including logistics.

The US$35 million figure covers only the first six months of operations, and WHO says the plan will be reviewed as risks change. The full document is available on the <a href="https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/who-global-preparedness-and-response-to-el-nino-related-public-health-risks-and-emergencies-strategic-preparedness-and-response-plan" target="_blank" rel="noopener">WHO El Nino response plan page</a>.

## Why El Nino Is a "Risk Multiplier" for Health

El Nino is a natural warming of the central and eastern Pacific Ocean that shifts rainfall and temperatures across the world. The WMO expects the current event to become very strong, and its State of the Climate 2026 report says El Nino "is expected to act as a risk multiplier for health."

The health risks listed by the WMO and WHO include:

- Heat related illness and deaths, and worsening of heart, lung, kidney and mental health conditions.
- Mosquito borne diseases such as malaria, dengue, chikungunya, yellow fever and Zika, which may spread into new areas, including higher altitudes.
- Cholera and malnutrition, judged to be at very high risk in both drought and flood affected places.
- Breathing problems from wildfire smoke and poor air quality.
- Injuries and food insecurity linked to floods and drought.

Teresa Zakaria, who leads risk reduction and climate change work in WHO's humanitarian operations, said the most severe effects will be felt where climate shocks meet weak health systems, poor access to care, hunger, displacement and conflict. The WMO noted in September that more than 150 million people already face acute food insecurity worldwide, a number expected to rise.

## Heat Exposure in India and South Asia

The WMO's latest climate report gives a clear picture of what extreme heat already means for this region. It says about 146.5 million people in India were exposed to extreme heat during a heatwave in April 2026. In neighbouring Nepal, a Himalayan glacier collapse and the flash flood that followed in late August killed more than 1,300 people.

For Indian readers, the diseases on WHO's watch list are familiar. Dengue, malaria and water borne infections already put heavy pressure on hospitals in many states. WHO's advice to protect older people, children, outdoor workers, people with long term illness and those living in poorly ventilated homes applies strongly to Indian cities, where buildings and roads trap heat. ## What Governments and Families Can Do Now

WHO and the WMO want health departments to work directly with weather services so that forecasts reach hospitals and local officials in time and in a form they can use. They also call for stronger disease surveillance and rapid response teams. WMO Secretary General Celeste Saulo has urged governments to "treat extreme heat as the predictable public-health risk that it is."

At the household level, simple steps help: staying hydrated and avoiding outdoor work in the hottest hours, using mosquito nets and clearing standing water, and drinking safe, boiled or treated water during floods. Checking on elderly neighbours during heat spells can also save lives.

## A First Test of Climate Health Planning

WHO's decision to grade El Nino as an emergency marks a shift from reacting after disasters to preparing in advance. With the event expected to peak around the end of 2026 and the Southern Hemisphere entering its hottest season in early 2027, the coming months will show whether early warnings can turn into fewer illnesses and deaths.
