Ten years after the government withdrew old Rs 500 and Rs 1,000 notes, India holds far more cash than it did before the note ban. Currency in circulation stood at Rs 43.16 lakh crore in late October 2026, nearly 143 percent higher than on November 8, 2016, according to RBI and finance ministry data reported by Business Standard. This has happened even as UPI grew into one of the largest real-time payment systems in the world. The anniversary has revived a simple question: why has cash refused to fade away?

## What Happened on November 8, 2016

Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced the move at 8 pm on November 8, 2016. From midnight, the Rs 500 and Rs 1,000 notes stopped being legal tender. An RBI assessment in March 2017 found that these notes made up 86.9 percent of currency in circulation, so most of the country's cash became invalid overnight.

The stated goals were to fight black money, fake currency and the funding of illegal activity, and to push people toward digital payments and the formal economy. The short-term result was a severe cash shortage that hit shops, small businesses and households for weeks.

## How Much Cash India Holds Now

The data shows that the dip after the note ban was temporary. Currency in circulation was Rs 17.77 lakh crore on the day of the announcement, based on finance ministry figures. By the week ending October 23, 2026, it had reached Rs 43.16 lakh crore, up 13.7 percent from a year earlier.

A narrower measure, currency with the public, tells the same story. Business Today, citing Indian Express and RBI data, reports that it fell from Rs 17.97 lakh crore on November 4, 2016 to about Rs 7.8 lakh crore in January 2017. By October 15, 2026 it had climbed to Rs 42.55 lakh crore. In calendar year 2026 alone, it rose by Rs 5.26 lakh crore, or 14.1 percent, up to October 16.

| Measure | Before note ban | Latest |
|---|---|---|
| Currency in circulation | Rs 17.77 lakh crore (Nov 8, 2016) | Rs 43.16 lakh crore (Oct 23, 2026) |
| Currency with the public | Rs 17.97 lakh crore (Nov 4, 2016) | Rs 42.55 lakh crore (Oct 15, 2026) |
| Currency to GDP ratio | 12.1% (FY16) | About 12% (FY26) |

At the end of 2025-26, currency in circulation was Rs 41.68 lakh crore, an increase of 11.9 percent. The rise of Rs 4.44 lakh crore in that year was the biggest jump since 2017-18, when the system was refilling after demonetisation.

## Cash Compared With the Size of the Economy

Raw numbers grow as the economy grows, so economists watch the currency to GDP ratio instead. This ratio dropped from 12.1 percent in 2015-16 to 8.7 percent in 2016-17 because of the withdrawn notes. It then rose steadily and peaked at about 14.4 percent in 2020-21, when people stocked up on cash during the Covid-19 lockdown for food and medical bills.

For 2025-26, Business Standard calculates the ratio at about 12 percent, using nominal GDP of Rs 346.36 lakh crore. In other words, cash relative to the economy is back near where it was before the note ban. Business Today notes that India still sits below Japan, where the ratio is over 16 percent, but above China at 10.2 percent and the United States at 7.3 percent.

## UPI Boom by the Numbers

The digital side of the story has changed beyond recognition. UPI, run by the <a href="https://www.npci.org.in/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">National Payments Corporation of India</a>, processed 17.8 million transactions in 2016-17. In 2025-26 it handled 241.62 billion transactions worth about Rs 314 lakh crore.

Key highlights:

- **September 2026:** 24.07 billion UPI transactions worth Rs 29.37 lakh crore, with 756 banks live on the platform.
- **Share of digital payments:** UPI made up about 84 percent of digital payments in 2025-26, according to government data.
- **Small payments:** 86 percent of person-to-merchant UPI payments in 2025-26 were below Rs 500.
- **Wider digital payments:** Retail digital payment volume rose from 71.77 billion in 2021-22 to 221.68 billion in 2024-25, according to the finance ministry.

## Why Indians Still Keep Cash

An RBI research paper from 2023, titled "Cash versus Digital Payment Transactions in India: Decoding the Currency Demand Paradox", offers an explanation. It found that people now use cash less for day-to-day buying, but more as a store of value and a safety cushion for emergencies. Higher incomes also tend to raise cash holdings. So a family may pay for groceries by UPI while still keeping notes at home for a hospital visit or a wedding.

This helps explain why both numbers can rise together. Digital payments have taken over small, frequent transactions, while cash has kept its role as savings and backup. ## A Cashless Goal Still Out of Reach

A decade on, India has become a world leader in digital payments but not a cashless economy. Cash is back to its pre-2016 share of GDP, and it is still growing faster than 13 percent a year. The debate over whether demonetisation achieved its aims will continue, but the data makes one thing clear: Indians have added UPI to their wallets without giving up their notes.
