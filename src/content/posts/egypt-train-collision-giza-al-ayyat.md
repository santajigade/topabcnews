Two passenger trains collided in the al-Ayyat district of Egypt's Giza province on Sunday, November 8, killing at least seven people and injuring more than 110, according to Egypt's Health Ministry. A train running between Giza and Fayoum struck the rear of another train travelling from Cairo to Aswan, and the impact pushed carriages off the track. President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi has ordered a quick investigation, and the public prosecutor is examining the technical causes.

## How the al-Ayyat Crash Happened

Al-Ayyat lies on the southern edge of greater Cairo, roughly 50 km from the capital along the main line that runs down the Nile valley. According to Egypt's Transportation Ministry, the train heading to Fayoum ran into the back of the Aswan-bound train on the same stretch of track.

Reports from the scene describe at least one train derailing, with overturned carriages coming to rest beside a countryside road. The ministry confirmed a derailment but did not say which of the two trains left the rails. One of the train drivers was among those killed, according to an Associated Press report carried by <a href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trains-collision-egypt-giza/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CBS News</a>.

No official cause has been announced so far. Investigators will be looking at signalling, train speeds and communication between the two crews, the usual focus in rear-end collisions, but authorities have not published any findings.

## Death Toll Rises Through the Day

The casualty figures climbed as rescue work continued. Early statements from the Health Ministry put the toll at four dead and 88 injured. Later on Sunday, the ministry raised the figures to at least seven dead and more than 110 injured, as reported by <a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/8/train-crash-kills-four-injures-dozens-near-egypts-cairo-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Al Jazeera</a>.

More than 70 ambulances were sent to carry the injured to nearby hospitals. The Egyptian Railway Authority sent emergency teams and heavy cranes to lift the damaged coaches and clear the line, saying it wanted services back on their normal timetable as soon as possible.

## Sisi Orders Probe and Accountability

President Sisi offered condolences to the families of the victims and directed state agencies to provide medical care to the injured. He also said that anyone found to have been negligent would be held accountable once the investigation is complete.

Egypt's public prosecution office has opened its own inquiry into the technical reasons behind the collision. In past rail accidents, such inquiries have looked at whether staff followed signals, whether equipment was working, and whether maintenance schedules were met. Officials have not said when the results of this probe will be shared.

## A District With a Long History of Rail Disasters

Al-Ayyat has a painful place in Egypt's railway history. In 2002, a fire on a crowded train carrying holiday travellers south of Cairo killed more than 370 people, the country's deadliest rail disaster. In 2009, collisions in the same area killed dozens more.

The district has seen smaller accidents in recent years as well. This September, two freight trains collided there, injuring a driver and his assistant. Elsewhere in the country, a derailment in 2021 killed 11 people and injured 98, and in August 2025 a passenger train derailment left at least three dead and more than 90 injured, according to Al Jazeera.

## Egypt's Ageing Rail Network Under Pressure

Egypt's rail system is one of the oldest and largest in Africa and the Middle East, and it is heavily used by a population of about 110 million people. For many families, especially in Upper Egypt, trains are the cheapest way to travel between towns and the capital.

Over the past decade, the government has spent billions of dollars on transport, including new highways and high-speed rail lines. However, much of the older passenger network, including its signalling and rolling stock, has not yet been fully upgraded. Accidents with casualties remain common, and each serious crash brings renewed calls for faster modernisation of the existing lines rather than only building new ones.

The challenge is familiar to many large rail networks that carry millions of daily passengers on ageing tracks. ## What Comes Next for Victims and Rail Services

The immediate focus is on treating the injured, identifying the dead and restoring traffic on one of Egypt's busiest corridors, which links Cairo with Fayoum and the cities of the south. Railway officials have said they aim to reopen the line quickly once the wreckage is removed.

The larger question is what the investigation finds. If the probe points to signalling or communication failures, pressure will grow on the government to speed up safety upgrades on older routes. For now, families in Giza and Fayoum are waiting for answers about how two trains came to be on a collision course on a line that has already seen some of the worst tragedies in the country's history.
