A federal court in Boston has thrown out the Trump administration's rule that would have limited how long international students can stay in the United States on an F-1 visa. On November 16, the US District Court for the District of Massachusetts vacated the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) rule that replaced "duration of status" with fixed admission periods capped at four years. The court called the rule "arbitrary and capricious," so the old system stays in place. Indian students, the largest group of foreign students in America, have the most riding on that.

## What the Court Threw Out

DHS published the final rule in the <a href="https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/17/2026-14439/establishing-a-fixed-time-period-of-admission-and-an-extension-of-stay-procedure-for-nonimmigrant" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Federal Register on July 17, 2026</a>, with an effective date of September 15. It covered F-1 students, J-1 exchange visitors and most I visa holders from foreign media.

A coalition of higher education groups and unions, including NAFSA, the Presidents' Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration, the American Federation of Teachers and the United Auto Workers, sued on August 18. On September 14, a day before the rule was to take effect, the judge paused it. The November 16 order goes further and cancels the rule outright.

According to a summary of the ruling reported by ICEF Monitor, the court found that DHS:

- did not properly weigh costs against benefits
- did not back its fraud and national security reasons with enough justification
- ignored less burdensome options, such as improving the existing SEVIS tracking system
- did not answer major public comments properly
- went beyond its legal authority in limiting academic study

The court wrote that the law "simply does not grant DHS such broad and open-ended authority" to weaken the F-1 programme.

## Duration of Status vs the Vacated Rule

| Point | Duration of status (still in force) | Vacated DHS rule |
|---|---|---|
| Length of stay | As long as you keep status and make normal progress | Programme length, capped at 4 years |
| Longer courses | No extension needed just because time passed | Extension of stay application to USCIS |
| Grace period after course or OPT | 60 days | 30 days |
| Changing level | Allowed under existing rules | Only a move to a higher degree after finishing one level |
| Graduate students changing major | Allowed under existing rules | Not allowed |
| Language courses | No special cap | 24 months in total |

DHS argued that duration of status gave officers too few checkpoints and allowed "pay-to-stay" schemes and very long enrolments. The court found DHS had not justified the changes on those grounds.

## Which Indian Students the Cap Would Have Hit

The Open Doors 2026 report, released on November 16 by the Institute of International Education, counted 339,435 Indian students in the US in 2025-26. That is 6% fewer than a year earlier, but India stayed the top source country for a third year, ahead of China with 254,458.

The breakdown by stage:

- **OPT: 182,201 Indians, up 27%.** More than half of all Indian students in the US are on Optional Practical Training, the work period after a degree. The cap covered post-completion work too: Harvard's guidance said students already in the US on the effective date could stay until their I-20 end date or the end of post-completion work authorisation, whichever was later, but no more than four years from that date.
- **Graduate students: 116,609, down 34%.** Under the rule, anyone whose programme ran past four years would have needed a USCIS extension to finish it.
- **Undergraduates: 39,711, down 1%.** Undergraduates in their first year would also have been barred from changing major or level except in extenuating circumstances.

Across all countries, new international enrolments fell 18% to 228,123 in 2025-26, according to the same report.

## A Checklist for Indian F-1 Students Right Now

1. **Nothing to file.** You do not need to apply for an extension of stay just because you have been in the US longer than four years. Keep your status valid and make normal academic progress, as before.
2. **Watch for an appeal.** The government may appeal, and the ruling holds unless a higher court stays or reverses it. Harvard's international office says the rule has no immediate effect on visas or start dates unless that happens.
3. **Talk to your international office before big changes.** The ruling adds no new benefits and removes none of your existing duties, so check with your school's international student office before you change programme, transfer or add a degree, especially while an appeal is possible.

You can read the full timeline on the <a href="https://www.hio.harvard.edu/visas-immigration/ds-rule-change" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Harvard International Office page on the D/S rule</a>, and what the ruling means for F-1 holders in <a href="https://www.businesstoday.in/nri/visa/story/us-f-1-visa-update-international-students-can-stay-beyond-four-years-without-extensions-568284-2026-11-19" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Business Today's report on the ruling</a>.

## What Could Happen Next

ICEF Monitor reports that the US government is widely expected to appeal, though no appeal had been confirmed when this article was written. If a higher court stays or reverses the November 16 order, the fixed four-year periods, the 30-day grace period and the limits on switching programmes in the table above could come back. Until then, F-1 students stay on duration of status.
