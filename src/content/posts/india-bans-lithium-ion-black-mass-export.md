India has stopped the export of lithium-ion battery "black mass", the metal-rich powder left after used batteries are broken down. The Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT), which works under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, changed the export policy for this material from "Free" to "Prohibited" with immediate effect, as reported on November 11, 2026. The step means black mass made in India can no longer be shipped abroad freely, and will have to be processed within the country or held back until further orders.

## What the DGFT Notification Says

The change applies to goods listed under ITC (HS) code 85491400. This code covers waste and scrap of primary cells, primary batteries and electric accumulators, including spent batteries. Within this, the notification targets unsorted waste and scrap that does not contain lead, cadmium or mercury, and identifies this material as lithium-ion battery black mass.

According to the notification quoted by ANI, the policy "is amended from 'Free' to 'Prohibited' with immediate effect, until further orders." It was issued under Sections 3 and 5 of the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992, read with the Foreign Trade Policy, 2023. Exporters can track such policy changes on the <a href="https://www.dgft.gov.in/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DGFT website</a>.

### What Is Not Covered

As reported by ThePrint, the order is not a blanket ban on every type of battery or battery-related material. It is aimed at the specific category described above. Battery scrap that contains lead, cadmium or mercury falls outside the description used in the notification.

## What Black Mass Is and Why It Is Valuable

When used lithium-ion batteries from phones, laptops, electric vehicles and energy storage systems are dismantled, crushed and processed, they turn into a dark, powder-like material. This is called black mass. It may look like waste, but it carries several costly metals:

| Material in black mass | Main use |
|---|---|
| Lithium | Battery cells |
| Cobalt | Battery cathodes |
| Nickel | Battery cathodes |
| Manganese | Battery cathodes |
| Graphite | Battery anodes |

Recyclers can pull these metals out through further chemical treatment, mainly a process called hydrometallurgy, which uses liquid solutions to separate and purify metals. The recovered metals can then be turned into compounds used to make new batteries.

## Why India Has Been Exporting This Powder

The government has not given a detailed reason in the notification itself. However, the issue was flagged earlier by the Ministry of Mines. In October 2025, a Ministry release reported by <a href="https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/business/india-pushes-for-e-waste-recycling-to-recover-critical-minerals" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The Tribune</a> noted that much of India's black mass was being exported because the country had limited capacity to process it at home. It also said only a few Indian companies run complete systems that convert battery scrap all the way into metals.

According to the same release, India generates about 1.75 million tonnes of e-waste and around 60,000 tonnes of spent lithium-ion batteries every year.

### The Recycling Push Behind the Move

The export ban fits into a wider set of steps aimed at building a domestic supply of critical minerals:

- In September 2025, the Cabinet approved a Rs 1,500 crore incentive scheme for critical mineral recycling as part of the National Critical Mineral Mission.
- The scheme offers up to Rs 50 crore to large recyclers and up to Rs 25 crore to smaller ones, rewarding those who actually extract lithium, cobalt and nickel.
- Customs duty on lithium-ion battery scrap imports was removed in the 2025-26 Union Budget.
- The Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) framework requires producers to ensure collection and recycling of battery waste.

The government has also recently approved support for critical mineral processing, which we covered in our report on the <a href="/critical-mineral-processing-scheme-lithium-nickel/">Rs 2,814 crore critical mineral processing scheme</a>.

## What It Means for Recyclers and the EV Sector

For companies that only collect, dismantle and crush batteries, the ban closes the option of selling black mass to overseas buyers. They will now need to sell to Indian refiners that can carry out metal recovery. For recyclers that already run hydrometallurgy plants, the move could mean more raw material staying within the country.

For the wider battery and electric vehicle industry, a steady domestic flow of recovered lithium, nickel and cobalt can reduce dependence on imported raw materials over time. India's growing battery storage plans, including the <a href="/energy-storage-vgf-scheme-rs-14500-crore/">energy storage VGF scheme</a>, also depend on battery materials.

### Key highlights

- DGFT moved lithium-ion battery black mass (HS 85491400, unsorted, without lead, cadmium or mercury) from Free to Prohibited for export.
- The change took effect immediately and applies until further orders.
- Black mass contains lithium, cobalt, nickel, manganese and graphite.
- India produces about 60,000 tonnes of spent lithium-ion batteries a year, as per a 2025 Ministry of Mines estimate.

## Keeping Battery Metals at Home

The ban is a clear signal that India wants the value locked inside old batteries to stay in the country. Its success will depend on how quickly domestic refining capacity grows, so that black mass collected in India can actually be turned into usable metals rather than piling up in storage.
