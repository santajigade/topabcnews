SpaceX has caught the upper stage of its giant Starship rocket with the mechanical arms of its launch tower for the first time. The ship returned to Starbase, Texas, on Saturday, November 14 (US time), about 22.5 hours after it lifted off from the same pad on Flight 15. The catch is a major step towards a fully reusable rocket, which SpaceX sees as the key to cheaper and more frequent spaceflight. The mission was not a complete success, though: the Super Heavy booster aborted its own catch attempt and was destroyed over the Gulf of Mexico.

## How Flight 15 Unfolded From Launch to Catch

Starship lifted off from Starbase at 5:47 pm EST on Friday, November 13, which was 4:17 am IST on November 14. It was the 15th full test flight of the vehicle and used the newer Starship V3 design, with Super Heavy Booster 22 and Ship 42. For the first time, SpaceX planned to recover both stages at the tower.

The ship fired its six Raptor engines and reached orbit roughly 26 minutes after launch, according to <a href="https://www.space.com/news/live/spacex-starship-flight-15-live-updates-nov-14-2026-starship-first-double-landing-attempt" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Space.com's live coverage of the flight</a>. Within about 1 hour and 17 minutes, it had released 26 Starlink V3 satellites.

Flight controllers then passed up an early return option that would have ended the mission with a splashdown near Hawaii. A second check, about 20 hours into the flight, also gave a "go" for the catch. After a planned 10-second deorbit burn on one engine, the ship re-entered the atmosphere and was caught by the tower's "chopstick" arms at about 4:20 pm EST, or 2:50 am IST on Sunday, November 15.

## Why Catching the Ship Matters

SpaceX has already caught Super Heavy boosters on three earlier flights, but the upper stage had never been recovered this way. Until now, ships either broke up or splashed down at sea. Catching both parts of the rocket and flying them again is the core of SpaceX's plan to cut costs and launch more often.

SpaceX commentator Dan Huot summed up the mood on the webcast, saying, "We caught a Starship, and now everything changes." He also called the ship catch "the missing link" in the company's reuse plans.

The flight was also the longest Starship mission so far, and only the second time the vehicle has reached orbit. Flight 14 in September was the first. According to <a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/15/spacexs-starship-captured-by-launchpad-arms-in-dramatic-first" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Al Jazeera's report on the landing</a>, SpaceX said systems including the heat shield performed well. Engineers used ground cameras and cameras on the deployed Starlink satellites to inspect the shield during the flight. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman described the catch on X as "almost magic," while Elon Musk congratulated his team.

## What Went Wrong With the Super Heavy Booster

The booster did not share the ship's success. After stage separation, it began its return to the launch site, but one of its 33 Raptor engines may have dropped out before the boostback burn. SpaceX's Kate Tice said early signs showed the burn ended early because of a low fuel mass alarm.

The booster's guidance system then found it was out of position for a catch and called off the attempt. Its flight termination system was triggered over the Gulf of Mexico. SpaceX first pointed to guidance, navigation and control, but later said that system worked as designed. The root cause is still under investigation, which means the first double catch will have to wait for a future flight.

## The Moon, Mars and What Comes Next

Starship is central to NASA's plans to return astronauts to the Moon. The space agency is paying billions of dollars for a version of Starship to land crews on the lunar surface under the <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NASA Artemis programme</a> later this decade. SpaceX also describes the vehicle as its ship for eventual travel to Mars.

A rocket that can return to its pad and fly again could change how often such missions are launched. SpaceX also expects to begin flying Starship from Kennedy Space Center in Florida within the next few months, according to Al Jazeera.

For Indian readers following space news, the contrast with other programmes is worth watching. ISRO is working to recover from setbacks of its own, as covered in our report on the [upgraded PSLV third stage test](/isro-pslv-third-stage-ground-test-success/). Reusable launch vehicles are a long-term goal for many space agencies, and SpaceX's progress raises the bar for everyone in the field.

### Key highlights

- Starship's upper stage (Ship 42) was caught by the Starbase tower for the first time.
- The ship spent about 22.5 hours in orbit, the longest Starship flight so far.
- 26 Starlink V3 satellites were deployed during the mission.
- Super Heavy Booster 22 aborted its catch after a shortened boostback burn and was destroyed.
- SpaceX is investigating the booster problem before the next flight.

## A Big Step, With One Piece Still Missing

Flight 15 proved that a Starship upper stage can come back from orbit and be caught at its launch pad, something no rocket had done before. The booster failure shows the system is still a work in progress. If SpaceX can repeat the ship catch and bring the booster home on the same flight, it will have shown the full reusability it has been chasing since the first Starship test in 2023.
