The winds that circle the North Pole high in the stratosphere reversed direction on 10 November 2026. That meets the usual test for a major sudden stratospheric warming, and no such event has been recorded this early in a winter. The previous earliest came on 28 November, a date reached in both 1968 and 2025, according to CNN. When it began, meteorologists told AFP its effect on ground-level weather was uncertain. A historically strong El Nino in the Pacific is pulling the other way.

## Update (25 November 2026)

**The warming happened.** Atlanta News First, citing NOAA's record of these events going back to 1958, reported that the winds about 19 miles (roughly 30 km) up reversed on 10 November, nearly three weeks earlier in the season than a 30 November 1958 event in one historical dataset. The UK Met Office confirmed on 18 November that a sudden stratospheric warming "has taken place". It said the disturbance that began it in late October was significantly stronger than any earlier early-season case in ECMWF data going back to the 1940s.

**The cold so far has been modest.** CNN reported a blast of Arctic air over North America and Europe from 11 November, with peak cold expected on 12 or 13 November. Nearly everywhere east of the Mississippi was expected to see its coldest weather since spring, 7 to 14 degrees Fahrenheit below mid-November averages, with widespread frost across the eastern US and northwest Europe. Forecasters said it would not be extreme and would ease the following week. In the UK, the Met Office said on 12 November that Arctic air had brought frost for most places. Snow was mainly a risk for higher ground in northern Scotland.

**El Nino has grown stronger.** NOAA's Climate Prediction Center kept its El Nino Advisory in its 12 November statement. Sea surface temperatures in the Nino-3.4 region were 2.5 degrees Celsius above normal, up from 2.1 degrees in October. CPC expects a strong to very strong El Nino through January to March 2027, then a weakening in early 2027.

## Polar Vortex, Jet Stream and SSW: The Terms Explained

These terms often get mixed up in headlines. Definitions below follow the Met Office.

| Term | What it is | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Stratospheric polar vortex | A ring of very strong westerly winds circling a pool of very cold air over the Arctic in the northern winter | Roughly 10 to 50 km up, in the stratosphere |
| Sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) | Polar stratosphere temperatures rise sharply over a few days and the vortex winds weaken. In a major event they reverse direction | Mid stratosphere, around 30 km |
| Jet stream | The band of fast winds lower down that steers weather systems. It tends to be strong and straight when the vortex is strong, weaker and wavier when it is disturbed | Below the stratosphere |
| El Nino | Unusual warming of the tropical Pacific that shifts weather patterns worldwide | Ocean surface, measured in the Nino-3.4 region |

The <a href="https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2026/what-is-the-stratospheric-polar-vortex-and-how-does-it-affect-uk-weather-" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Met Office explainer on the stratospheric polar vortex</a> stresses that an SSW does not automatically mean cold weather. Some are followed by notable cold spells, others have little lasting effect, and the strongest impacts may land elsewhere in Europe or the Northern Hemisphere.

## How Often an SSW Brings Cold to the Ground

According to Reuters, about two-thirds of SSW events are followed by colder and drier than normal conditions in northern and northwestern Europe. NOAA's analysis of 42 major warmings from 1958 to 2023 found that parts of the eastern US, northern Europe and Asia were on average colder in the month after these events.

With a lag of about two to six weeks, late November and December are the period to watch. Adam Scaife, head of long-range prediction at the Met Office, told Reuters that November collapses have happened before, but "never this early in the month".

## Why El Nino Could Blunt the Cold

NOAA's October discussion said chances were high for a historic El Nino stronger than any since records began in 1950. Carbon Brief reported in September that the daily Nino 3.4 anomaly reached 3.11 degrees Celsius, above the previous record of 3.08 degrees set in November 2015.

Scientists quoted by CNN disagree on whether El Nino or the vortex will win. Andrea Lopez Lang of the University of Wisconsin-Madison called El Nino a big bully that may keep cold within typical winter ranges. Amy Butler of NOAA expects a higher chance of warm temperatures over the northern half of the US. Judah Cohen of MIT disagreed and said the cold depends on how the vortex behaves. He called the mid-November chill the appetizer, with a higher risk of cold in eastern North America and northern Europe in late November or early December.

Scientists also do not agree on the cause. Reuters reported that El Nino can send atmospheric waves toward the pole that weaken the vortex, but that effect is usually strongest later in winter. Unusually warm seas north of Scandinavia and western Russia may also have played a part. Simon Lee of the University of St Andrews told CNN it is unclear whether El Nino contributed. The same El Nino prompted the <a href="/who-el-nino-health-emergency-grade-3-plan/">WHO's grade 3 health emergency plan</a>.

## What to Watch Before Christmas

Butler told CNN the vortex should rebuild quickly, so the disruption may not last long. Lopez Lang said another disruption later this winter would not be surprising, and the next one might be the one that brings the impacts. Reuters noted there is no clear evidence yet of a long-term climate change trend in the vortex.

For Indian students, tourists and business travellers heading to North America or Europe in December, the practical step is to check local forecasts in the week before flying, since the two to six week window runs through the holiday season.
