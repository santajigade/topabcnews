The polar vortex, the ring of powerful winds that spins high above the North Pole every winter, is weakening far earlier in the season than scientists have ever recorded. The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) said in its updated November outlook that a disruption could develop within days and turn into a sudden stratospheric warming, an event it described as very rare for November. Meteorologists told AFP on Friday, November 6, that the early disruption is unusual, but its effect on winter weather across the Northern Hemisphere remains uncertain.

## What the Polar Vortex Actually Is

The polar vortex is a large area of low pressure and cold air sitting more than six miles (about 10 km) above the Arctic, in the layer of the atmosphere called the stratosphere. When it is strong and centred over the pole, it acts like a fence that keeps the coldest air locked in the far north.

The vortex normally builds up through autumn as the Arctic cools and usually holds firm until around December or January. It can be disturbed when large waves of air from lower in the atmosphere push upward into it, making it wobble, slow down or stretch. Meteorologist Jonathan Erdman of <a href="https://weather.com/2026/10/27/forecast/national/polar-vortex-early-season-stretch-super-el-nino-us" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The Weather Channel</a> compared the stretching to pulling both ends of a rubber band.

## Why a November Breakdown Is So Unusual

The stretching is often driven by a sudden stratospheric warming, when temperatures high above the pole jump by 50 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit or more within a few days. These events usually happen in the depth of winter. According to experts cited by Scientific American, the previous earliest recorded weakening of this kind came on November 28, 1968, which means the current event could be weeks ahead of anything seen before.

Judah Cohen, a climatologist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology who studies the vortex, called it a big event "because it's so unprecedented." He has said the weakening could be without precedent this early in the season.

A disturbance in the stratosphere does not always reach the ground. Sometimes it slowly works its way down and bends the jet stream, the fast river of air that steers weather systems. When that happens, pockets of Arctic air can spill south into North America, Europe or Asia. But this does not happen every time, and the timing can lag by weeks.

## El Nino Could Pull Winter the Other Way

The bigger influence on this winter may be El Nino, the periodic warming of the Pacific Ocean. NOAA says El Nino is now in full swing, is expected to stay strong at least into late winter, and has a high chance of becoming the strongest since records began in 1950.

At NOAA's October 15 winter outlook briefing, Jon Gottschalck of the Climate Prediction Center said a strong El Nino usually favours a stronger, steadier vortex and fewer Arctic outbreaks. He still expects cold spells at times, but more warmer than normal days overall. NOAA said El Nino could offset any cooling effect from the vortex in the eastern United States and still points to a milder November there.

Forecasters at The Weather Company's energy team said any blocking patterns caused by the weaker vortex will likely struggle against El Nino's warming push. They added that the stratospheric warming favours impacts in Europe more than North America in late November and early December, though the US East Coast could turn cooler. The forecasting firm Atmospheric G2 has predicted the warmest November to January period on record for the US.

## Lessons From January's Deep Freeze

Memories of last winter explain the public interest. In January 2026, a lobe of Arctic air brought a severe freeze to the eastern US and Canada. Parts of northern Minnesota fell to minus 43 degrees Fahrenheit on January 24, and Indiana set record lows, while Greenland's west coast recorded its warmest January on record. That contrast shows how a disturbed vortex can send cold to one region while another warms.

### Key highlights

- NOAA says a sudden stratospheric warming in November would be very rare
- The previous earliest recorded weakening was on November 28, 1968
- Effects of a disruption can last up to two months, according to NOAA
- A record strength El Nino may limit cold outbreaks in North America
- Europe may feel effects sooner than the US, forecasters say

## Should Anyone Be Worried

Scientists stress that the event is fascinating rather than alarming. NOAA's guidance is that there is no cause for alarm, but people in affected regions should be ready for colder spells, check local forecasts and keep home and car emergency kits stocked. For Indian students, tourists and business travellers heading to North America or Europe over December, it is a good reason to watch local forecasts before flying.

## What Scientists Will Watch Next

Over the next few weeks, researchers will track whether the disturbance travels down from the stratosphere and alters the jet stream.
