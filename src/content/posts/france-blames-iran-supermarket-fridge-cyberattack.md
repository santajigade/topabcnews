France on Monday, November 9, publicly blamed a cyber unit tied to Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) for an October attack that took remote control of refrigeration systems in hundreds of French supermarkets. Temperatures inside fridges and freezers were pushed to about 50 degrees Celsius overnight, forcing stores to throw away fresh and frozen food. Foreign Minister Jean-Noel Barrot said the attack "will not go unanswered" and promised to seek sanctions at the European level.

## How Hackers Took Over Supermarket Fridges

According to French investigators, the attack happened in the early hours of October 12, after starting overnight on October 11. The hackers gained access to internet-connected refrigeration control systems and remotely raised temperatures. The <a href="https://apnews.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Associated Press</a> reported that temperatures stayed at around 50 degrees Celsius for roughly six hours.

About 700 of France's roughly 10,000 supermarkets were affected, AP said. Retailers reported that millions of euros worth of food was spoiled, and in some stores the heat sparked fires or melted equipment. Investigators said the attackers used Farsi and IP addresses linked to devices located in Iran.

## Who France Says Is Responsible

France's national cybersecurity agency, ANSSI, attributed the attack to a group called Shahid Kaveh, which it described as an offensive cyber unit linked to the Revolutionary Guards. Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau has opened a formal judicial investigation into suspected organised cybercrime carried out to serve a foreign power, namely Iran.

Prosecutors are seeking international arrest warrants for the leader of Shahid Kaveh and his two deputies, though their names have not been made public. Speaking on the sidelines of G7 talks in Paris, Barrot called it an attack of "unprecedented scale and aggressiveness". In an interview with <a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/9/france-accuses-iran-of-cyberattack-on-european-fridges" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Al Jazeera</a>, he described it as the first large-scale cyber sabotage operation on French soil.

The French Foreign Ministry condemned the attack in the strongest terms, and the Iranian ambassador in Paris was summoned. No response from Tehran was included in the reports from AP and Al Jazeera.

## Other European Countries Reported Similar Problems

France may not be the only target. Supermarkets in Belgium, Estonia, Ireland, Luxembourg and Sweden reported sudden overnight temperature spikes in fridges and freezers on November 2, affecting dozens of stores and a food distribution hub. Those incidents appear to have stemmed from cyberattacks, according to AP, though no formal attribution has been announced for them.

AP also reported that an attempt last week to raise temperatures at several French blood banks to dangerous levels was stopped before it caused damage. ANSSI has linked earlier attacks on France since 2020, including those on the satirical newspaper Charlie Hebdo in 2023 and on the organisation of the 2024 Paris Olympics, to a different IRGC-linked group called Shahid Shoostari.

## Why Connected Fridges Are a Security Risk

The case shows how ordinary building systems can become targets once they are connected to the internet. Commercial fridges, freezers, air conditioning and lighting in large stores are often managed remotely so that technicians can monitor them and fix faults quickly. If those control panels are exposed online or protected by weak passwords, attackers can change settings without ever entering the building.

Unlike data theft, this kind of sabotage causes direct physical and financial damage: spoiled stock, fire risk and empty shelves. For food retailers, that also raises safety questions, since food kept at the wrong temperature must be destroyed.

Security experts generally advise operators of such systems to keep them off the open internet, use strong unique passwords and multi-factor login, apply software updates, and separate operational networks from office and guest Wi-Fi.

## What It Means for India and Other Markets

Many supermarkets, cold storage units and pharma cold chains in India also rely on remotely monitored equipment. The French case is a reminder for Indian retailers and food distributors to check whether their refrigeration and building controls can be reached from the open internet. Organisations in India can follow security advisories from <a href="https://www.cert-in.org.in/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CERT-In, the national cyber security agency</a>, and report incidents to it.

## What Comes Next Between Paris and Tehran

France will now push for European Union sanctions against those it holds responsible, while the judicial investigation and arrest warrant requests move forward. The dispute adds to tensions between Iran and Western governments, which have also been rising over regional security, including [US moves to block Iran's trade routes](/us-task-force-iron-gate-iran-land-routes/). How the EU responds, and whether other affected countries name a culprit for the November 2 incidents, will be the next things to watch.
