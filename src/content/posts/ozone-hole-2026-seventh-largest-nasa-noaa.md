The hole in the ozone layer over Antarctica grew unusually large in 2026, ranking as the seventh biggest in 48 years of monitoring. Scientists at the US agencies NASA and NOAA released their yearly measurements in early November. The hole reached its peak on September 18, when it covered about 27.1 million square kilometres. The main reason was the weather high above the South Pole, not a rise in harmful chemicals, and the scientists say the ozone layer is still on course to heal later this century.

## How Big the 2026 Ozone Hole Became

According to <a href="https://www.noaa.gov/news-release/noaa-nasa-release-measurements-of-2026-ozone-hole" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NOAA's official release on the 2026 ozone season</a>, the hole peaked at 10.5 million square miles, or 27.1 million square kilometres. That is roughly 90% of the largest hole ever seen, which was recorded on September 9, 2000.

Across the peak period from September 7 to October 13, the hole averaged around 25 million square kilometres. NOAA said that is more than twice the area of the United States. The ranking of seventh largest is based on this seasonal average area.

### 2026 ozone season at a glance

| Measure | 2026 figure |
|---|---|
| Peak size | 27.1 million sq km |
| Date of peak | September 18 |
| Seasonal average (Sep 7 to Oct 13) | About 25 million sq km |
| Rank by seasonal area | 7th largest in 48 years |
| Lowest ozone reading | 105 Dobson units on October 17 |
| All-time lowest reading | 92 Dobson units in October 2006 |

## A Large Hole, but Not a Very Deep One

Size is only half the story. Scientists also track how thin the ozone gets, measured in Dobson units. Balloon-borne instruments launched from the South Pole recorded the year's lowest overhead ozone at 105 Dobson units on October 17. Ground instruments at the South Pole, which have collected data since 1962, confirmed that reading.

The record low over the South Pole is 92 Dobson units, set in October 2006. The University of Colorado's research institute CIRES, which works with NOAA, said in <a href="https://cires.colorado.edu/news/noaa-nasa-rank-2026-ozone-hole-among-10-largest-record" target="_blank" rel="noopener">its summary of the findings</a> that this year's hole was large but not as deep as the record holes of 10 to 20 years ago. Scientists also pointed out that the hole has been forming later in the season in recent years, which they see as a sign of healing.

## Why Weather Made This Year Worse

The researchers blamed an unusually strong and large polar vortex, the band of powerful winds that circles Antarctica in winter and spring. Inside this cold, sealed area, chlorine and bromine compounds destroy ozone once sunlight returns.

Paul Newman, a senior scientist at the University of Maryland who has long led NASA's ozone research team at Goddard Space Flight Center, said the vortex "has extended ozone depletion in the Antarctic this season."

Amy Butler, a research scientist at NOAA's Chemical Sciences Laboratory, explained that large scale weather waves, which usually disturb the vortex, were weak in August and September. As a result, the strongest winds stayed several degrees of latitude away from the pole. That widened the area where ozone could be destroyed. Readers may recall that the polar vortex in the northern hemisphere has been behaving unusually too, as we reported in our story on the <a href="/polar-vortex-weakening-record-early-2026/">record early weakening of the polar vortex</a>.

## Montreal Protocol Still Doing Its Job

Despite the large size, the agencies stressed that the long-term trend is positive. The 1987 Montreal Protocol, a global treaty that phased out ozone-damaging chemicals such as CFCs, continues to drive recovery.

Key points from the scientists:

- Stephen Montzka of NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory said chlorine from ozone-depleting substances in the Antarctic stratosphere has fallen by about a third from its peak in the 1980s
- Newman estimated the hole could have been about 3.5 million square kilometres larger if chemical levels were still at year 2000 levels
- Scientists expect the Antarctic ozone hole to recover by the 2070s

Irina Petropavlovskikh of CIRES said the long South Pole records give the baseline needed to "detect and quantify the recovery".

## How the Ozone Hole Is Tracked

Monitoring uses both satellites and ground stations. Satellites have watched the ozone layer since 1979. This year, data came from NASA's Aura satellite, the NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 satellites, and Suomi NPP, which NASA and NOAA operate together. These are backed up by balloon launches and Dobson instruments at the South Pole, where CIRES researchers spend the long polar winter taking measurements.

## Why This Matters Beyond Antarctica

The ozone layer shields life on Earth from harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun, which is linked to skin damage, eye problems and harm to crops and ocean life. Although the hole forms over Antarctica, the success of the Montreal Protocol is often cited as proof that global environmental agreements can work. That message carries extra weight this month, as countries, including India, meet at the <a href="/cop31-opens-antalya-climate-finance-dispute/">COP31 climate talks in Antalya</a> to argue over how to cut emissions and pay for climate action.

The 2026 numbers show that year to year weather can still produce a large ozone hole. But the falling chlorine levels and later formation of the hole suggest the repair work started nearly four decades ago is paying off, even if full recovery is still about 50 years away.
