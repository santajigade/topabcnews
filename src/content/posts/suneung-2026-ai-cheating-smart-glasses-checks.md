## Update (24 November 2026): Education Minister Resigns Over Exam Rule Chaos

South Korea's Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin resigned on Sunday, November 22, three days after the exam, and President Lee Jae Myung accepted the resignation the same day. <a href="https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/education/20261122/education-minister-resigns-amid-backlash-over-csat-anti-cheating-measures" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The Korea Times</a> reports that Lee accepted it within an hour. Choi said last-minute changes to exam preparations had "caused inconvenience to the public", and that the test had still ended without major incident.

The complaints were about timing. According to The Korea Times, the ministry announced 8,400 extra staff two days before the exam, about 1,800 of them for detecting smart devices and 6,600 as hallway proctors, and told students to cover clothing buttons with stickers. A day before the exam it banned items that had been allowed until then, including computer-readable marking pens, correction tape, erasers and mechanical pencil lead. Newly assigned proctors got rushed online training the day before. Korea JoongAng Daily reports that proctors checked glasses on exam day, that emergency phone alerts went out nationwide three times during the test, and that the ministry caught 111 suspected cases of cheating with electronic devices.

Lee has asked Prime Minister Han Seong-sook to oversee the follow-up. In a post on X, reported by JoongAng Daily, Lee said exam fairness "can never be compromised", promised a full inspection of how the CSAT and other exams are run, and said he would order a review of adding identification marks to question papers so that photographed or leaked papers can be traced. No successor had been named in the reports we read. The original report from November 17 follows below.


South Korea will inspect the glasses of every student sitting the Suneung, its national college entrance exam, on Thursday, November 19. The move follows a scandal that broke on November 15, when the top scorer of this year's national tax accountant exam admitted he had cheated with a hidden camera and a generative AI tool. More than half a million students take the Suneung, and officials now fear that AI devices small enough to hide in a frame or a button could undermine the country's most important test.

## The Confession That Rattled Korea's Exam System

The case centres on Han Won-seok, 34, who ranked first in both rounds of the tax accountant exam. According to the <a href="https://abcnews.com/Technology/wireStory/ai-cheating-fears-spook-south-korea-ahead-high-138171058" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Associated Press report carried by ABC News</a>, he scored 397.5 out of 400 in the April multiple choice stage and 663 out of 800 in the July essay stage. His results had drawn suspicion for weeks.

In a video posted on his lawyer's YouTube channel, Han said he photographed the questions with a miniature camera, sent the images to an AI tool and read the answers on a smartphone. He sent a written confession to Seoul police, who are investigating. The finance ministry is moving to suspend his qualification, and the Prime Minister has called for his results to be cancelled and for him to be barred from the exam for five years.

The scandal did not come out of nowhere. Students were caught with AI smart glasses in an English test in June and in some university essay exams in October, The Next Web reported.

## What Changes Inside the Exam Hall on November 19

The Education Ministry and Prime Minister Han Seong-sook have set out a list of steps, with more measures due to be announced today, November 17. Here is what has been confirmed so far, based on reports in The Korea Herald, Kyunghyang Shinmun and Seoul Economic Daily:

- **Glasses checks every period:** All glasses worn by candidates will be inspected in each testing period. Proctors will look for built-in cameras, thick frames, lens displays, charging ports and power buttons.
- **A trained checker at every centre:** At least one specially trained official will be posted at each of the 1,312 exam centres.
- **Devices handed in:** Students must hand over all smart devices, and proctors must check during the exam that none remain.
- **Wi-Fi under review:** The science ministry is studying whether public Wi-Fi at test sites can be switched off.
- **Watching for gestures:** Proctors have guidance on spotting hidden cameras through movements around sleeves, collars and buttons.
- **Escalation chain:** Doubtful cases go up from centre staff to regional offices and a joint ministry response centre.
- **Post-exam checks:** Officials will investigate possible cheating after the test with the help of police.

Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin has rejected calls from lawmakers to postpone the exam, saying a delay would cause "even a larger disruption" for students.

## Why Korea Cannot Simply Jam Signals or Search Bags

Some of the most obvious fixes are blocked by law or practicality. The table below sums up what officials have said about each option.

| Option | Status for this year's Suneung | Reason given |
|---|---|---|
| Inspecting all glasses | Adopted | Smart glasses tend to have thick frames, speakers and displays |
| Collecting smart devices | Adopted | Prime Minister's directive |
| Switching off public Wi-Fi | Under study | Science ministry consulting |
| Signal jamming | Ruled out | Radio Waves Act restricts jammers; could hit emergency and nearby communications |
| Searching all candidates | Ruled out | Searches of bodies and belongings need each student's consent |
| Metal detectors for everyone | Limited use only | Already used on some students, such as after restroom visits |
| Thermal cameras | Not adopted | Privacy concerns and stress on test takers |
| Radio frequency detectors | Planned for next year | Being developed for next year's exam |

A teachers' union has warned that the ministry must not rely "solely on proctors' eyes and judgment." The ministry has also said it wants to raise the bar on caught cheaters retaking the Suneung from one year to three years or more, which needs a change in law.

Each year Korea also delays office hours and stock market opening on exam day, and pauses flights during the English listening section, AP reported. This year's test is the last under the current common and elective subject system, with an integrated format starting from the 2028 academic year.

## How India's Exam Law Compares

India has faced its own exam integrity crises, mostly over paper leaks rather than AI devices. The central law covering exams run by the Centre and its agencies, the Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024, was amended this year. According to the amended text on <a href="https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20100/1/A20241.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">India Code, the government's official legal database</a>, the changes took effect on July 31, 2026.

| Offence under India's Act | Before amendment | After July 31, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Using unfair means | 3 to 5 years jail, fine up to Rs 10 lakh | 5 to 10 years jail, fine up to Rs 50 lakh |
| Service provider involvement | Fine up to Rs 1 crore, 4 year ban | Fine up to Rs 5 crore, 8 year ban |
| Organised exam crime | Minimum 5 years, fine at least Rs 1 crore | 7 to 10 years, fine at least Rs 10 crore |

The contrast is useful. India has leaned on heavy criminal penalties aimed at leak gangs and service providers, while Korea's immediate problem is an individual candidate quietly using a device in the hall. Korea's answer this week is mostly about detection, and its own officials admit the technology is not there yet. Indian agencies such as the NTA, which recently opened applications for <a href="/nta-exam-observers-pool-centre-empanelment/">a new pool of exam observers</a>, may well face similar questions as AI glasses become cheaper.

## What to Watch After Thursday

Officials will review reports from all 1,312 centres after the exam. Choi has promised that AI cheats "would be caught without fail and punished severely," while admitting current tools fall short. Korea is also debating whether makers should make AI glasses flash or beep when in use, an idea exam boards elsewhere, including in India, will be watching.
