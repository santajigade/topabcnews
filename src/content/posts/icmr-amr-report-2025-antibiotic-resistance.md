Several of the antibiotics Indian doctors prescribe most often no longer work against a large share of common hospital infections, according to the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR). Its Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance and Research Network (AMRSN) Annual Report 2025, covering samples collected from January to December 2025, was reported this week as the world marks <a href="https://www.who.int/news-room/events/detail/2026/11/18/default-calendar/world-amr-awareness-week-2026" target="_blank" rel="noopener">World AMR Awareness Week</a>, which runs from November 18 to 24.

## A Bigger Network This Year

The 2025 report draws on 1,13,008 culture-positive samples from 30 large tertiary-care hospitals. For the first time it also includes 34 secondary-care hospitals, 12 of them district hospitals, which added 20,529 samples.

The four most common bacteria in the large hospitals were:

- E. coli: 24.4% of samples
- Klebsiella pneumoniae: 16.9%
- Pseudomonas aeruginosa: 12.2%
- Acinetobacter baumannii: 9.5%

Each figure in the report is the share of samples that responded to a drug in lab tests, so a low number means most samples did not respond to it, as The South First explains. The report does not measure treatment failure, recovery or deaths.

## Drug by Drug: Where Antibiotics Still Work

**E. coli**, a frequent cause of urinary infections, responded to cefotaxime in only 18.4% of samples, to ceftazidime in 24.7% and to ciprofloxacin in 9%. Levofloxacin worked in 18.2%. Low sensitivity showed up in outpatient samples too, which suggests resistance is present outside hospitals. Two older drugs held up: fosfomycin worked in 96.5% of samples, the best result of any antibiotic tested, and nitrofurantoin in 79.1%.

**Klebsiella pneumoniae**, linked to bloodstream and lung infections, responded to carbapenems, the powerful drugs kept in reserve, in only about 35% to 40% of samples. In ICU samples the figure fell to around a quarter: 26.1% for ertapenem, 24.9% for imipenem and 26.3% for meropenem. Genes linked to carbapenem resistance, OXA-48 and NDM, were found in some samples.

**Acinetobacter baumannii** was resistant to carbapenems in 91.1% of tested samples, which the report says severely limits treatment options.

**Staphylococcus aureus** was methicillin-resistant (MRSA) in 56.8% of samples from large hospitals. In the new secondary-care network, the MRSA share was 86% in hospitals with 500 or more beds and 46.4% in those with fewer than 200. Vancomycin, teicoplanin and tigecycline worked against all MRSA samples tested there.

**Typhoid** bacteria still responded well to azithromycin (99.4%) and ceftriaxone (96.6%), but ciprofloxacin worked in just 2% of samples.

**Diarrhoea-causing bacteria** showed some of the worst numbers. Shigella flexneri responded to ciprofloxacin in 7.5% of samples and Shigella sonnei in 8.9%. For diarrhoea-causing E. coli, ciprofloxacin worked in 0.7% and cefixime in 4.6%.

The report also carried data on newborns for the first time. In babies up to 28 days old, Klebsiella pneumoniae caused 25.1% of bloodstream infections, and several of the bacteria responded poorly to third-generation cephalosporins, which makes newborn sepsis harder to treat.

## How the Numbers Compare With Earlier ICMR Reports

ICMR has published these surveillance reports every year. The table below sets the 2025 figures against the <a href="https://www.icmr.gov.in/icmrobject/uploads/Report/1763981012_icmramrsnannualreport2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">2024 report</a> and the 2022 report, as covered by Down To Earth. The network, the hospitals and the sample mix change from year to year, so read this as a broad direction rather than an exact trend.

| Measure | 2022 report | 2024 report | 2025 report |
|---|---|---|---|
| Culture-positive samples | 1,07,053 (21 hospitals) | 99,027 | 1,13,008 (30 hospitals) plus 20,529 from 34 secondary hospitals |
| Acinetobacter resistant to carbapenems | About 88% | 91% (meropenem) | 91.1% |
| Klebsiella sensitive to carbapenems | 40% to 46% | 31.2% (imipenem), 35.1% (meropenem) | About 35% to 40% |
| MRSA share of S. aureus | Not stated in coverage | Nearly 53% | 56.8% |
| E. coli sensitive to ciprofloxacin | 12% | 10.7% | 9% |
| Most common bacterium | Not stated in coverage | E. coli, 26.2% | E. coli, 24.4% |

Over three reports, carbapenem resistance in Acinetobacter has stayed around nine in ten samples, and the 2025 report says carbapenem resistance is "becoming an increasingly important component" of the overall picture.

## What ICMR Wants Doctors and Patients to Do

The report calls for better use of antibiotics, stronger infection control in hospitals and treatment based on local resistance patterns. Its specific advice includes:

1. Avoid certain antibiotics for kidney infections and for urinary infections with fever, because resistance to them is high.
2. Do not use carbapenems routinely for typhoid.
3. Choose antibiotics based on lab sensitivity tests, not past experience, and test in severe or complicated diarrhoea cases.
4. Improve hand hygiene, sterilisation of instruments and cleaning of hospital surfaces and equipment.

For patients, The South First's reading of the report comes down to one rule: do not pick antibiotics yourself. The right drug depends on where the infection is, how severe it is, the patient's condition and the sensitivity results. ## The Global Toll of Drug Resistance

WHO says drug-resistant bacteria are linked to more than 4.7 million deaths a year and that AMR could cause up to 39 million deaths by 2050 if nothing changes. This year's awareness week theme is "One Health, one action: Prevent AMR now."
